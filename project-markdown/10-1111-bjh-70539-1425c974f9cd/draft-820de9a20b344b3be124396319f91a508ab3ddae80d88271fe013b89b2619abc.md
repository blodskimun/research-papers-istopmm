The challenge of defining and understanding monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance

There have been several recent reviews and commentaries focusing on monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS). This research letter aims to add to the growing interest in MGUS by focusing on gaps in evidence, research priorities and ongoing research such as the Iceland screens, treats or prevents multiple myeloma (iStopMM). MGUS is a common, asymptomatic, clonal expansion of plasma cells that is a low-­risk precursor condition to other plasma cell disorders most commonly multiple myeloma (MM). It is distinguished from a second MM precursor condition, smouldering myeloma (SMM) by the level of plasma cell infiltrate (\<10% vs. 10%–59% plasma cells) and/or paraprotein level, but both have an absence of myeloma-­related organ damage by the SLiM-­CRAB criteria that defines MM1,2 (Table S1). The initial clinical management of patients with MGUS relies on risk stratification (Table 1) and the exclusion of organ damage related to the clone3 including rare monoclonal gammopathies of clinical significance4 followed by sub-sequent monitoring.5

Currently, there are major challenges both with the definition and in miscategorising of individuals with plasma cell disorders. For most individuals, these issues may have no consequence and categorisation is not indicated clinically if the individual is likely to have low-­ or intermediate-­risk MGUS, based on paraprotein levels, serum free light chain (SFLC) levels and absence of relevant symptoms as it requires an invasive bone marrow procedure. For a minority, a mis-­categorisation can contribute to catastrophic late diagnosis,6,7 impact mental health, impact insurance and restrict access to certain treatments and trials. Individuals at par-ticular risk are patients with MM presenting with low-­level paraproteins and individuals whose non-­specific symptoms are attributed to other causes. Critically, access to cross sec-tional imaging, whole body magnetic resonance imaging (WB-­MRI) and positron emission tomography-­computed tomography, is also limited which inevitably leads to late diagnosis of patients with bone disease.8–10 Categorisation also relies on estimating the percentage of plasma cells (or lymphoplasmacytic cells if IgM paraprotein) in the bone marrow trephine biopsy in a disease characterised by patchy marrow involvement11,12 with normal haemopoiesis also of variable cellularity.13 The iStopMM study14 screened people over 40 years old and those found to have a presumed MGUS were randomised into three groups: no follow-­up, routine follow-­up as per international guidelines and more intensive follow-­up, undergoing bone marrow biopsies and imaging with WB-­MRI. The key finding was that the prevalence of individuals with \>10% plasma cells (SMM) was 0.5% (0.67% in men, 0.39% in women) and considerably higher than expected. Individuals with low-­risk MGUS by blood work had a 6.7% risk of finding smouldering myeloma diagnosed by bone marrow but a lower than expected rate of progression to active myeloma compared to previous studies of SMM.15 Thus, the iStopMM, screen-­detected SMM cohort represents a biologically more indolent spectrum than clinically detected cohorts, with direct implications for affecting risk stratification and progression estimates. The iStopMM team have released a calculator that estimates the probability of finding \>10% plasma cells within the marrow (https://​istop​ mm.​com/​riskm​odel/​) and this has recently been validated in more ethnically diverse populations (S16, S17). Mass spectrometry can identify monoclonal proteins with high sen-sitivity at a high frequency in the general population (S18).

The PROMISE screening study used the QIP-­MS/EXENT assay within high-­risk populations (African Americans and close family relatives, who have a parent, sibling or child with MM) and found the prevalence of monoclonal gammopathy to be 43%. Those below the limit of detection for gel electrophoresis and immunofixation (\<0.2 g/L) were termed monoclonal gammopathy of indeterminate potential (MGIP) (S19). MGIP appears to be a biologically distinct group which is frequently transient, often with IgM or mul-ticlonal isotypes, and a very low risk of progression (S19). Current methods for measuring SFLCs cannot differentiate between polyclonal and monoclonal FLCs and the presence of a monoclonal light chain MGUS must be inferred from a skewed SFLC ratio. This is challenging as there is uncertainty about the most appropriate reference ranges especially with renal impairment (S20, S21), reactive polyclonal elevations and age-ing. It has been reported that the risk of progression for light-­ chain MGUS is lower compared with conventional MGUS, but such results should be interpreted with caution given that the changes arising from renal or polyclonal effects will often re-sult in a false-­positive MGUS diagnosis. The iStopMM study

published a revised set of reference intervals and a new definition of LC MGUS (S22, S23) which have been validated in both Chinese and Israeli populations (S24, S25) and supported by multiple independent external studies in systemic amyloidosis (S26, S27). Mass spectrometry represents a potential advance as it can identify monoclonal light chains, but this technology is not yet clinically available (S18). The identified population of individuals with MGUS does not represent the true MGUS population, which can only be understood through prospective screening, such as the iStopMM study.14 While it appears that the progression rates for screened (screened MGUS) and clinically detected MGUS (clinical MGUS) are similar (S28), there remains uncertainty regarding the association of MGUS with other diseases (S29). People with MGUS are only detected when they are seeking medical advice for unrelated health issues generating a large number of spurious clinical associations (S29, S30). The iStopMM study has compared comorbidities between screened and clinical MGUS, finding a much higher incidence of comorbidities in clinical MGUS (S31) and that most reported associations are likely spurious,

However, there is an association of MGUS with venous thrombosis, unrelated to the monoclonal protein level (S33–S36), and there is an increased risk of infection in SMM (S37). The biology of MGUS is poorly understood and research is challenging due to the need for an invasive biopsy to collect low levels of clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow. The initiating IgH immunoglobulin locus translocations on chromosome 14q32 and hyperdiploidy are present, but so too are subclonal heterogeneity and the full spectrum of myeloma-­associated mutation classes (S38, S39). The class and number of abnor-malities such as large copy number variations and single nucle-otide variations increase with progression. Increased APOBEC (apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic polypeptide-­like) mutational activity, structural variations such as chromothrip-sis, p53 loss and activation of MYC (transcription factor) may be important in progression (S40). Two studies have identified high-­risk versus low-­risk genetic signatures (S38, S41) distinct for stable and progressive precursor MM conditions, but cru-cially these need testing in larger prospective studies. MGUS and myeloma are associated with changes in the microenvironment (S43), but most reported changes are es-tablished at the MGUS stage. Changes in T-­cell anti-­tumour immunity with disease progression were shown over 15 years ago (S43, S44) indicating loss of tumour immunosurveillance, but further evidence for this has been lacking. The majority of people may have had MGUS for decades (S45) with a paraprotein often targeted against a pathogen or inflammatory sphingolipid (S46, S47). Glycosylation occurs in 5% of light chain variable domains normally (S48), but this is slightly commoner in AL amyloidosis (S49, S50) and N-­linked glycosylation may be linked to progression from MGUS (S49). The 2005 ‘Mayo model’ defined risk groups for MGUS based on type of paraprotein (non-­IgG vs. IgG paraprotein), paraprotein level (\>15 g/L) and FLC ratio (abnormal vs. normal) (S51). The 40% of individuals with low-­risk MGUS have a risk of progression of \<5% at 20 years and there is a de-bate around monitoring of such individuals (S21). The Mayo MGUS high-­risk group had an absolute risk of progression at 20 years of only 27% so defining true high-­risk MGUS has proven impossible. The PANGEA model for MGUS and SMM integrates serial measurements (S52) unlike the Mayo and PETHEMA models (S53) (Table 2). There is a need for novel blood-­borne biomarkers, and currently, there is interest in soluble B-­cell maturation antigen levels (S54). There is a benefit in detecting people at the precursor stage as MM is one of the worst cancers for delayed diagnosis with high early morbidity and mortality and increasing evidence to suggest a benefit in treating patients with high-­risk smouldering myeloma (S56, S57). However, criteria for validating a screening programme are poorly met (S58, S59). Selected screening of high-­risk groups is more attractive but in the PROMISE study, the MGUS rate was only 6% (19). iStopMM and SECURE studies have both shown no reduction in quality of life (S60, S61) following an MGUS diagnosis. Various models exist for monitoring (S62) but need improvement, lacking both comparison and health economic analysis.

Understanding the biology and natural history of precursor diseases is challenging, especially its possible disease associations, disease progression and identifying high-­risk disease. This is hampered by a lack of research with only one prospective unselected screening study iStopMM and one selected screening study PROMISE still in follow-­up. MM precursor disease has been overlooked by research (see Table 2) and healthcare systems, and despite the challenges, this needs to change.
