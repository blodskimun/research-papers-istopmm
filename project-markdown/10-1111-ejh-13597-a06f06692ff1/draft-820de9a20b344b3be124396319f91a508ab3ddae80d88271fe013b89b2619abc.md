### Abstract

High proportion of patients with multiple myeloma suffer from comorbidities which

may alter clinical management. Therefore, our aims were to evaluate the prevalence

of comorbidities and their impact on survival. We included patients diagnosed with

multiple myeloma 1990-­2013 in Sweden and all diagnoses from each patient from

1985\. A total of 13 656 patients with multiple myeloma were included in the study,

thereof 7404 (54%) had comorbidity at diagnosis. The risk of death was increased for

those with one comorbidity at diagnosis compared to those without any comorbid-

ity (hazard ratio = 1.19; 95% confidence interval:1.14-­1.25); this risk was higher for

those with two (1.38; 1.30-­1.47) and three or more comorbidities (1.72; 1.62-­1.83).

Furthermore, the risk of death was increased in patients with prior history of can-

cer, arrhythmia, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, chronic lung

disease, psychological disease, peptic ulcer, neurological disease, peripheral vascu-

lar disease, chronic kidney disease, dementia, and inflammatory bowel disease. This

large study shows that over 50% of multiple myeloma patients have a comorbidity

at diagnosis and survival decreased with increasing numbers of comorbidities. This

emphasizes the importance of comorbidities when evaluating patients and deciding

on treatment strategies for individuals with multiple myeloma.

1 \| INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a plasma-­cell malignancy that affects

approximately 160,000 individuals annually world-­wide.1 The me-

dian age at diagnosis is around 70 years, 5%-­10% of patients are di-

agnosed below 50 years of age, with a lower prevalence of young

patients in population-­based studies.2,3 With an aging population

and prolonged survival, the incidence and prevalence of MM are

increasing4-­7 and elderly patients are more likely to suffer from

comorbid conditions.8,9

Comorbidities may influence cancer detection, treatment, pro-

gression, and thereby prognosis of each patient.10,11 In MM patients,

comorbid diseases may lead to lower performance status, decreased

quality of life, and life-­threatening conditions.12,13 We have recently

New therapeutic agents with different mechanisms of action,

such as lenalidomide, pomalidomide, daratumumab, and carfilzo-

elderly and frail patients are included.12 Patients with comorbid-

ities are typically not eligible for enrollment in clinical trials, and,

consequently, there is limited information available on comorbid-

ities in MM and implications on clinical outcomes. Therefore, it is

of great importance to evaluate comorbidity in MM, in particular

using real-­world data.

Aims of our study were to evaluate the prevalence of comor-

bidity and the effect of comorbid conditions on survival in a whole

population using real-­world data among more than 13 000 newly

diagnosed MM patients in Sweden over a period of 23 years.

2 \| METHODS

2.1 \| Cohort and registries

All residents of Sweden are, at birth or immigration, assigned a

unique personal identification number that is used in government-­

maintained nationwide health care and population registers,

whereby medical record linkage is possible with a high degree of

accuracy.

The Swedish Cancer Registry is a centralized, nationwide reg-

istry that contains information on patients diagnosed with a ma-

lignant disorder in Sweden since January 1, 1958 (completeness

\>93% for MM patients).22 Using this registry, patients diagnosed

with MM from January 1, 1990, to December 31, 2013, were

identified.

Using International Code of Diseases (ICD)-­10 and ICD-­9 codes,

a total of 21 disease groups were identified (Table S1). The formation

of these groups is explained below. Information on each patient was

gathered from the Swedish Patient registry, which contains inpa-

tient data from 1987 as well as information on outpatient visits from

2000.23 All chronic diseases that were diagnosed up to 10 years be-

fore the diagnosis of MM were included in the analysis. Diagnosis

of comorbid diseases had to be made before the MM diagnosis or a

maximum of 30 days after diagnosis with the exception of inflamma-

tory bowel disease, renal, and rheumatological disease. These con-

ditions had to be diagnosed at least half a year before as symptoms

of MM might mimic these diseases and lead to a wrong diagnosis.

In a sensitivity analysis, we removed all diagnosis up to four weeks

prior to the MM diagnosis and risk estimates for each disease re-

mained the same (data not shown). Information on prior cancer di-

agnoses was obtained from the Swedish Cancer Registry. All cancer

diagnoses up to 10 years prior to diagnosis of MM were identified,

excluding benign tumors as well as cancer in situ.

End of follow-­up was December 31, 2013. The study period was

divided into two calendar periods, 1990-­1999 and 2000-­2013, be-

fore and after the introduction of novel treatment options for MM in

Sweden.6,24 Information on date of death as well as cause of death

was obtained from the centralized Swedish Cause of Death Registry.

In evaluating the cause of death, the data were split into those who

had MM listed as any cause of death and those without a MM diag-

nosis in the Cause of Death Registry.

2.2 \| Comorbidity

A total of 65 diseases were identified using ICD-­9 and ICD-­10 codes

from the Swedish Patient Registry, using both inpatient and outpa-

tient data. These diseases were grouped together according to pre-

viously published data, both the Charlson comorbidity index as well

as newly published study from Denmark.19,25 Certain diseases that

are not evaluated in the Charlson index were added to capture as

many comorbidities as possible, specifically those known to effect

survival in the general population as well as the survival of patients

with known cancer.14,26-­31 In total, psychological disorders, neuro-

logical conditions, arrhythmias, pancreatic disease, inflammatory

bowel disease, hypertension, and obesity were added. The diseases

listed were chronic in nature and typically need lifelong follow-­up

or treatment.

ICD-­9 and ICD-­10 codes were used to identify each diagnosis

in the corresponding groups, and these codes were based on ICD-­9

and ICD-­10 versions of the Charlson index.32 In order to group these

65 diseases together, we conducted a separate analysis where all

65 diseases were evaluated independently for their risk of death.

Diseases were grouped together according to their ICD-­10 classifi-

cation as well as the organ or organ systems that were affected by

the disease. After ICD and organ system classification, diseases were

grouped together if the risk of death was similar. A disease was only

added to a group if the risk was alike or did not change when the

disease was added to that specific category. Altogether, a total of

21 disease groups were defined. Table S1 shows the diseases groups

and which diagnosis were included with corresponding ICD-­9 and

ICD-­10 codes for each group.

2.3 \| Statistical analysis

We estimated the effect of comorbidities on the risk of death for

patients with MM using two different Cox regression models. The

first model included only the number of comorbidities present at

diagnosis and the second model included all of the 21 comorbidities

we identified. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, and year of

diagnosis with age and year of diagnosis as continuous variables.

We also looked at all subjects that died during the study period,

As the outpatient diagnosis was only available from the year

2000, a sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate if there was

any difference in the risk of each comorbidity between two calen-

an interaction term between each comorbidity and the time period

variable to the Cox model. For further comparison, we fitted a sepa-

ratios changed when outpatient diagnoses were excluded. To assess

the interaction between the risk of each comorbidity and age at di-

agnosis, we stratified the group into patients older and younger than

70 years of age and fitted a Cox model for each age group separately

as well as adding an interaction term between each comorbidity and

the age group variable.

Statistical significance was set at P \< .05, all confidence intervals

were 95%, and all statistical analyses were performed in R version

3.6.1.

3 \| RESULTS

In total, 13 656 patients were diagnosed with MM in the Swedish

Cancer Registry from the January 1, 1990, to the December 31,

2013\. The median age of diagnosis was 72 years (20-­99), and 7481

(54.8%) were male. Around 60% of patients were older than 70 years

at the time of MM diagnosis, and 63% of these older patients had a

comorbidity at diagnosis (Table 1).

A total of 7404 (54.2%) patients had one or more comorbidity at

MM diagnosis, and the most common comorbidities were hyperten-

sion (20.4%), cancer (11.3%), arrhythmia (11.3%), and chronic isch-

emic heart disease (9.2%). Other comorbidities and their prevalence

are shown in Figure 1. Of all patients, 3355 (24.6%) had only one

comorbidity listed at the MM diagnosis, 1922 (14.1%) had two co-

morbidities, and 2126 (15.6%) had three or more comorbidities at

diagnosis. More patients were diagnosed with MM and concurrent

comorbidities during the calendar period 2000-­2013 (61.7%) com-

pared to 1990-­1999 (38.3%), and patients with comorbidities were

older at the time of MM diagnosis, 75 years compared to 69 years of

age (Figure 1; Table 1).

The risk of death was significantly increased for those with a

known comorbidity at MM diagnosis (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.29-­

1.40). Having one comorbidity at diagnosis increased the risk of

death by 19% (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.14-­1.25), and having two and

three or more comorbid diseases at diagnosis increased the risk of

death incrementally (HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.30-­1.47 and HR = 1.72,

95% CI: 1.62-­1.83, respectively, Figures 1 and 2). The difference

between these four groups was statistically significant, P \< .001

(Figure 1).

The risk of death was significantly increased among patients

with 14 comorbidities out of the 21 that were defined (Figure 2).

The risk was highest for patients with dementia at MM diagnosis

(HR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.45-­2.04), followed by patients with chronic

heart failure (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.44-­1.66). The risk was signifi-

4 \| DISCUSSION

In this large population-­based study including over 13,000 patients

with MM diagnosed in Sweden over a period of 23 years, and de-

tailed information of all of their significant comorbidities, we dem-

onstrated that over 50% patients with a newly diagnosed MM have

at least one comorbidity at diagnosis. Importantly, our study shows

that MM patients with comorbidities present at diagnosis have 33%

higher risk of death compared to those without a comorbid condi-

tion. In addition, our results show that 14 disease categories are re-

lated to worse survival. These findings emphasize the importance of

comorbidities in MM, especially as frail patients with comorbidities

are often excluded from RCTs.33

The prevalence of comorbidity at diagnosis was 54% in the

current study, higher than in a recent study from Denmark that

only utilized diagnoses compatible with the Charlson comorbidity

index.19,25 Due to our thorough approach using registry data, we in-

clude important comorbidities that have, to our knowledge, not been

studied before, such as psychological diseases which are known to

decrease survival in the general population as well as among cancer

patients.27,31 The prevalence of each disease was similar to previous

studies, with hypertension being the most common comorbidity,

followed by heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and a previous malig-

nancy.11,17,19,34 In our study, patients with a known comorbidity were

older at the time of diagnosis compared to those without a comorbid

condition; however, a total of 40% were under the age of 70 years,

indicating the presence and importance of comorbidities in younger

patients.

Our findings demonstrate that having any comorbidity at MM

diagnosis, increases the risk of death by 34% compared to those pa-

tients without a comorbidity at diagnosis. Furthermore, there was

an apparent additive effect of the number of comorbidities on sur-

vival. Importantly, this relationship was preserved for both older and

younger patients, with a step-­wise increase in the risk of death for

each contributing comorbidity. The presence of comorbidity might

affect treatment choices and therefore reduce survival, as has been

shown in younger patients in Denmark, where a marked reduction

in high-­dose therapy with autologous stem cell transplant among

younger patients with many comorbidities was observed.19 This

underlines the importance of assessing each patient at diagnosis,

especially regarding the presence of comorbidities in the patients’

medical history, as patients with a known comorbidity might be more

vulnerable to treatment and prone to adverse effects which might in

turn influence the overall survival. The ongoing COVID-­19 pandemic

has even shed a light on the importance of comorbidities, where up

to 75% MM patients who got infected by the virus and admitted to

hospital had a comorbidity at diagnosis, and over 80% of patients

who died from COVID suffered from other diseases than MM.35

In the current study, 14 diseases were related to a higher risk

of death. Patients with dementia had the highest risk of death

(72% higher), followed by heart failure which increased the risk by

54%. This is in accordance with a prior smaller study19 among pa-

tients with MM as well as other studies the general population.36,37

Interestingly, in our study, having a history of a mental disorder in-

creased the risk of death 1.3-­fold. This, to our knowledge, has not

been reported before in MM patients, but survival among cancer

patients with psychological diseases is known to be decreased.31

Additionally, the risk was increased in those with a history of inflam-

matory bowel disease, peptic ulcer disease, neurological disease,

liver disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebro-

vascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, cancer, diabetes melli-

tus, and arrhythmia with a moderate risk increase, similar to previous

reports on MM patients14,19 as well as in patients with cancer.28-­30

Interestingly, history of peptic ulcer decreases survival in the gen-

eral population, which seems to be explained by frailty, treatment-­

related events, and comorbidities that go in hand with a history of

peptic ulcer.38 Similarly, the diagnosis of arrhythmia increases the

risk of death in the general population39,40; in the current study, this

was true for patients \<70 years when compared to \>70 years of age

probably reflecting more severe underlying heart disease-­causing

arrythmia in younger patients. History of ischemic heart disease did

not increase the risk of death in MM patients, reflecting advances in

treatment and survival in the past decades.41 Rheumatologic disease

did not increase the risk of death; however, in a sub-­analysis, the

risk of death was significantly increased for rheumatoid arthritis and

ankylosing spondylitis. To assess this further, these diseases were

used instead of the disease group of rheumatoid diseases. When

correcting for other diseases as well as age, year of diagnosis, and

gender, this risk became non-­significant. Taken together, the effect

of comorbidities on survival in MM patients underlines the com-

plexity of evaluating newly diagnosed patients, with the possibility

of both under-­treating by a dose-­reduction or increasing the risk of

treatment-­related conditions such as infection or thrombosis.

The presence of a kidney disease is well known to have an im-

pact on survival in MM, and it is often a challenge to distinguish

between a comorbidity or myeloma-­kidney disease.16,20 In our

study, we only included patients with a known kidney disease at

ney disease was the only comorbidity that was different between

these periods, and patients with chronic kidney disease had a sig-

nificantly lower risk of death in the more recent years. This dif-

ference remained when outpatient diagnosis where excluded. The

most likely explanation is that after 2000, more effective treat-

ment for MM emerged.4,6,24 Novel drugs were increasingly used in

Sweden, with up to 80% of patients receiving one of these drugs in

2013.7 In particular, bortezomib has been shown to reduce tumor

burden and improve renal response, and is the drug of choice in

renal failure.42 Immunomodulatory drugs such as lenalidomide (re-

quires dose-­adjustment according to creatinine clearance) and po-

malidomide are also safe for this patient group.42 Furthermore, the

management of chronic kidney disease and end-­stage renal disease

has improved in the last decades with a reduction in mortality rate

among these patients.43 Therefore, the decrease in risk of death

in this group can represent more aggressive and better treatment

of these individuals in the latter calendar period. To further evalu-

ate the effect of novel therapies, an analysis of the later calendar

period was performed, comparing the years 2000-­2006 to 2007-­

2013\. There was no difference in risk of death except for patients

with a known diagnosis of cerebrovascular disease where the risk

became non-­significant in the later calendar period. This correlates

to the recent trends in mortality and improved survival among

patients with cerebrovascular disease.44 However, there was no

other difference between these calendar periods, both in risk of

death and overall survival, despite a steep increase in the use of

novel treatments, such as the immunomodulary drugs in the later

era. This might be explained by the short follow-­up time of patients

and the impact of treatments need to be studied further as no in-

formation on individual treatments was available in this study.

In the cause of death analysis, 11% of patients did not have

MM listed as a cause of death which is similar to a recent European

study.45 Furthermore, a higher number of comorbidities at diagno-

sis gave higher odds of dying from other causes than myeloma. A

recent Spanish study46 on early mortality yielded similar results,

with comorbidities such as liver disease, respiratory disease, and

hepatitis C infection, all contributing to an early mortality. This

emphasizes even more the complexity of comorbidity at diagnosis

and the challenge it poses in relation to treatment decisions and

patient evaluation.

Our study possesses several strengths. First, it is nationwide and

includes all patients diagnosed with MM in Sweden over a period of

23 years. The data are collected from a stable population which has

access to standardized universal health care with high completeness

and validity of the Swedish Cancer Registry.22 The use of real-­world

data ensures that our cohort reflects a whole patient population.

Additionally, using the Swedish Patient Registry, we are able to cap-

ture every diagnosis both from inpatient data and from outpatient

registries for each patient in the years leading up to the MM diagno-

sis. As every individual has a unique identification number, we have

an extensive and accurate follow-­up for all patients included in the

study. Furthermore, the application of a register-­based cohort de-

sign minimized recall-­bias and the high number of patients included

yielded high statistical power.

The limitations of our study include lack of information on clini-

cal stage, laboratory data, and treatment of each individual, making

it difficult to evaluate if the presence of comorbidities influenced

individual treatment choices or was associated with higher risk of

adverse events. This is of particular importance among older indi-

viduals who have more comorbidities and do not receive a high-­

dose therapy with autologous stem cell transplantation. In addition,

outpatient data were only available from the year 2000, which

might underestimate the prevalence and risk of each comorbidity.

However, sensitivity analysis based only on patients diagnosed after

the year 2000 showed no difference between the calendar periods

(data not shown). Furthermore, information on obesity was based on

ICD diagnosis which is not widespread and not based on the body

mass index of patients. Unfortunately, we do not have access to this

data and it is therefore likely that we have not captured all obesity

diagnoses.

In conclusion, our results show that 50% of newly diagnosed

MM patients have at least one comorbidity and that it impairs

overall survival when compared to patients without a known co-

morbidity, even showing an increased impact relating to the num-

ber of comorbid conditions. Our results are clinically important as

we show that newly diagnosed MM patients with an underlying

comorbidity have a significantly shorter overall survival (median

3.8 vs 2.4 years, respectively) compared to newly diagnosed MM

patients without a comorbid condition. Furthermore, our results

address the significance of evaluating each patient at diagnosis

with proper risk stratification, including a detailed medical history

as the presence of comorbidity may increase the risk of adverse

effects and result in less aggressive treatment regimens, with both

leading to inferior survival in MM.
