### ABSTRACT

Multiple myeloma (MM) and related malignancies develop from an asymptomatic precursor condition, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined signiﬁcance (MGUS), detectable via blood testing. The beneﬁts and harms of population-based MGUS screening remain uncertain. We conducted a nationwide screening study for monoclonal gammopathies and a randomized trial of follow-up strategies in Iceland (Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents Multiple Myeloma; ClinicalTrials.gov identiﬁer: NCT03327597). In total, 75,422 (53% of those invited) were screened and those with MGUS (n 5 3,541) were randomly assigned to no notiﬁcation (arm 1), guideline-based follow-up (arm 2), or intensiﬁed follow-up (arm 3). Progression, symptom burden, and psychological well-being were compared between the control arm (arm 1) and intervention arms (arms 2 and 3). After a median follow-up of 4.5 years, screening led to a 27- fold increase in the detection of smoldering MM (8.6% v 0.3%; hazard ratio, 27.46 \[95% CI, 10.21 to 73.86\]; P \< .001), but active malignancy rates did not differ. Active MM and related malignancy were diagnosed 1 year earlier in the intervention arms with fewer symptomatic presentations and hospitalizations at diagnosis. MGUS notiﬁcation was not associated with adverse psychological outcomes. These ﬁndings demonstrate that population-based screening facilitates earlier detection of MM and expands access to early intervention without detectable psychological harm. Longer follow-up is required to determine the effects on survival and cost-effectiveness.

### INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma (MM) and other symptomatic monoclonal gammopathies are preceded by monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined signiﬁcance (MGUS), 1-3 an asymptomatic disorder with an annual progression risk to active malignancy of 0.5%-1%. 4,5 Follow-up is recommended for MGUS to detect progression before end-organ damage. This is particularly important in MM, where disease burden at diagnosis is associated with morbidity and mortality 6,7 and prior diagnosis of MGUS has been associated with lower disease burden and improved survival. 8-10 Initiating treatment at the intermediate precursor stage of high-risk smoldering MM (SMM) can delay progression to active MM and improve survival. 11-13 However, similar to MGUS, SMM is asymptomatic and thus rarely encountered in clinical practice and 93%-97% of patients with MM are diagnosed at a symptomatic stage when it is too late for early treatment. 8-10 Screening may present a way to detect these

precursors and extend the impact of early treatment to the wider MM patient population.

Screening is one of the cornerstones of oncology but is controversial and can carry signiﬁcant harm, 14 including overdiagnosis and overtreatment, health care resource use and ﬁnancial toxicity, and psychological distress. Despite the widespread implementation of cancer screening programs, the psychological effects of screening remain poorly studied. 15

The Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents Multiple Myeloma (iStopMM) study is a population-based MGUS screening study and a randomized clinical trial of follow-up strategies. To our knowledge, it is the ﬁrst clinical study designed to determine the beneﬁts and harms of population-based screening for monoclonal gammopathies. The primary outcome of the study is overall survival, which remains immature. Here, we report secondary outcomes on early clinical and psychological effects of screening.

### METHODS

All residents of Iceland, born in 1975 or before and alive in September 2016 (148,711), were invited to participate; 80,759 (54%) provided their informed consent, of whom 75,422 (93%) were screened for monoclonal gammopathy. Serum samples were collected between 2016 and 2020 in parallel with other blood testing ordered within the universal Icelandic health care system, supplemented by a targeted blood sample collection drive, with relatively stable accrual across the screening period (Data Supplement, Fig S1, online only). Samples were screened by serum protein electrophoresis (Helena Laboratories, Beaumont, TX) and free light chain (FLC) assay (Freelite, The Binding Site, part of Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, Birmingham, UK), with immunoﬁxation electrophoresis performed in cases of suspected M proteins or abnormal FLC testing. Those with newly detected apparent MGUS were randomly assigned to three study arms using block random assign-ment by M protein concentrations ≥1.5 g/dL and light-chain MGUS (Data Supplement, Table S1). 16,17 Arm 1 was the control arm where participants were not notiﬁed of the MGUS status and continued as if they had not been screened. Arm 2 and arm 3 were the intervention arms that were notiﬁed and offered clinical assessment and follow-up either using a risk-adapted use of imaging and bone marrow

### RESULTS

Participant ﬂow and characteristics are shown in the Data Supplement (Fig S2) and Table 1, respectively. Psychological outcomes were assessed using 11,216 questionnaires from 2,593 participants (73%). Throughout follow-up, PHQ-9 (depression) and GAD-7 (anxiety) scores were lower in the intervention arms, with small effect sizes (Cohen’s d 0.18-0.23), whereas satisfaction with life scores were similar across groups. Rates of severe depression, anxiety, or life dissatisfaction did not differ before or after 18 months from screening (Data Supplement, Table S6).

In this randomized controlled trial evaluating screening for monoclonal gammopathies including 75,422 participants, population-based screening led to a 27-fold increased detection of SMM and a decreased diagnostic delay for MM and related malignancies by 1 year, before the occurrence of severe end-organ damage. Screening was associated with decreased risk of MM and related malignancies in the age group between 40 and 70 years, which included 81% of those who received early treatment, but not overall. These ﬁndings show that screening facilitates earlier detection of MM and may signiﬁcantly delay or prevent progression. Although a shorter time to diagnosis does not by itself constitute clinical beneﬁt, the ﬁndings underscore that screening is a prerequisite for early intervention strategies in high-risk SMM to have a meaningful impact at the population level.

Few participants developed malignancy, and the ex-tensive testing and follow-up in both intervention arms raise questions about the economic viability of screening. Future work should focus on optimizing MGUS care and identifying those who do and do not beneﬁt from follow-up.

Based on more than 11,000 mental health assessments, notiﬁcation of MGUS was not associated with adverse psychological outcomes. Small differences favoring the intervention arms were clinically insigniﬁcant and may reﬂect bias from clinical contact. This study provides robust evi-dence that, within a system providing specialized care without individual ﬁnancial toxicity, MGUS diagnosis through screening does not affect overall mental health.

This study has several strengths, including the randomized design, wide recruitment criteria, high participation rate, and the use of high-quality registry data enabling virtually complete capture of disease progression, including for arm 1. Limitations include relatively short follow-up, reliance on

end points that do not directly capture direct beneﬁt and are susceptible to lead-time bias, exclusion of individuals with advanced disease at screening who beneﬁt the most, and a genetically and ethnically homogenous population.

In summary, in this randomized controlled trial evaluating monoclonal gammopathy screening, we found that screening led to a dramatic increase in the detection of SMM and earlier diagnosis of active MM, facilitating timely treatment when end-organ damage has not become clinically apparent. These beneﬁts were not at the cost of psychological distress. Longer follow-up is needed to determine whether early diagnosis translates into improvements in overall and disease-speciﬁc survival. Moreover, given the high number of MGUS cases relative to the improvements in early detection, it is unlikely that screening is economically viable in the current paradigm of MGUS care. Nevertheless, our ﬁndings highlight how screening, coupled with efﬁcient MGUS care with better risk-adapted approaches, could shift the clinical paradigm from diagnosing MM at advanced, symptomatic stages toward early detection, timely intervention, and potential disease prevention.
