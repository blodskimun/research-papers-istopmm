### Abstract

Objective: To evaluate temporal trends in survival and causes of death in patients

with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) in a nationwide study.

Methods: The cohort consisted of 13,009 Swedish CLL patients diagnosed 1982–­

2013\. Relative survival (RS) and excess mortality rate ratios (EMRR) with 95% con-

fidence intervals (95% CIs) were estimated using flexible parametric survival models.

Cause-­specific hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated for the linear effect of 10-­year

increase in year of diagnosis.

Results: The excess mortality decreased comparing 2003–­2013 to 1982–­1992

(EMRR = 0.53, 95% CI 0.48–­0.58). The 5-­year RS increased between 1982 and

2012 for patients \>51 years at diagnosis and improved for patients ≤51 years after

2002\. The rate of CLL-­specific deaths decreased over time (HR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.75–­

0.81). Compared to patients with no comorbidity, patients with 1 and 2+ Charlson

Comorbidity Index points had HR = 1.35 (95% CI 1.25–­1.45) and HR = 1.47 (95% CI

1.37–­1.57) for CLL-­related mortality, respectively.

Conclusion: Survival in CLL patients improved in the era of chemoimmunotherapy,

and this was largely explained by reduced CLL-­related mortality. The increased rate

of CLL-­related mortality in patients with comorbidities emphasizes the importance of

the newer and better tolerated targeted therapy.

1 \| INTRODUCTION

With the introduction of purine analogs, monoclonal antibodies, and

immunomodulatory drugs at the turn of the century, the response

rates in treatment for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) improved

compared to earlier treatment with chlorambucil.1–­4 The results

from clinical trials of these drugs are, however, inconsistent where

most,5–­8 but not all,9,10 research groups failed to show improvement

Novelty statement: 1. Few population-­based studies on temporal trends of survival and causes of death in CLL patients have been published, and the prognostic role of comorbidities in CLL has not been established. 2. Survival in CLL continues to improve in the era of chemoimmunotherapy, and this is mainly explained by decreasing mortality due to CLL itself. Comorbidities at CLL diagnosis are associated with an increased risk of CLL-­related and CLL-­unrelated mortality. 3. These findings underline the importance of considering effective treatment for CLL, even in patients with known comorbidities. Fortunately, these approaches have improved with the new targeted therapy.

The inconclusive results and selection bias of important earlier

trials underline the importance of population-­based studies to con-

firm the clinical benefit observed in clinical trials. Few population-­

based studies on temporal trends of CLL survival have been

published.14,21-­24 These have reported an improved survival in recent

decades, although the improved survival in the youngest patients

seems to have lagged during the era of chemotherapy,4 especially

for young male patients.25 A recent study on CLL patients diagnosed

1989–­2016 in the Netherlands, and thus in part covering the era of

targeted therapy, observed improved survival in all age groups be-

tween the latest calendar periods.26

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia is typically a disease of the

elderly27 where comorbid conditions are common28 and many

patients will not require CLL therapy for years following the diag-

nosis.29–­31 Therefore, it is important to evaluate the burden of CLL

itself in relation to comorbidities. The literature on specific causes of

death and the impact of comorbidities in CLL on survival is scarce. A

prospective single-­center study (n = 1,143) depicted that CLL pro-

gression was the cause of death in nearly half the patients28 and in a

Danish population-­based analysis based on 10,455 CLL patients, im-

proved survival in recent decades was mainly due to decreased risk

of death from CLL and hematological malignancies.25 The prognostic

role of comorbidities in CLL has not been established in population-­

based studies, and results from clinical trials and single-­center stud-

ies have either shown minimal12 or negative28,32 effect on survival.

To establish the temporal changes in survival, causes of death,

and the prognostic role of comorbidities in real-­world CLL patients,

we conducted a population-­based study with more than 13,000 CLL

patients diagnosed from 1982 to 2013 in Sweden.

2 \| METHODS

2.1 \| The Swedish nationwide registers, the patient cohort, and outcomes

Data were retrieved from Swedish nationwide registers, namely

the Swedish Cancer Register (SCR), the Swedish Cause of Death

Register, and the Swedish Patient Register. Linkage between regis-

ters was possible using the national registration number issued to all

Swedish residents.

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia patients diagnosed from 1982 to

2013 were identified in the SCR.33 The SCR was started in 1958 and

includes information on type of cancer, diagnosis date, and patient

characteristics for all primary malignancies diagnosed in Sweden.

Reporting is done by a physician and/or pathologist and is manda-

tory by law.

3 \| STATISTICAL METHODS

Data were analyzed using time to event analysis. The underly-

ing time scale was time since diagnosis, and all patients entered at

30 days after diagnosis (i.e., delayed entry). Patients were followed

until date of death, December 31, 2013, or ten years after diagnosis,

whichever came first.

Temporal trends in cancer-­related mortality were analyzed using

relative survival methods. Expected mortality rates, stratified on

year, sex, and age, were retrieved from the human mortality data-

base (www.morta​lity.org). For estimation of excess mortality rate

ratios (EMMR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), a flexible para-

metric relative survival model41 with 5 degrees of freedom (df) for

the baseline, assuming proportional excess hazards, was used. Both

univariable and multivariable; adjusted for age (≤50, 51–­65, 66–­80,

≥81), sex, and calendar year (1982–­1992, 1993–­2002, 2003–­2013);

models were fitted. To estimate temporal trends, the assumption of

proportional excess hazards was relaxed and a more complex model

was used. This model included sex, age, and calendar year (modeled

For each competing cause of death, HRs and 95% CIs for a ten-­

variables, and with time. Due to convergence issues, the dfs for the

time-­varying effects were 2 for CLL deaths and 1 for the remaining.

All analyses were performed using Stata version 15 software

(StataCorp). Modeling and predictions were done using the stpm242

and multistate43 packages.

Approvals were obtained from the Ethical Review Board in

4 \| RESULTS

4.1 \| Demographic features

In total, 13,009 patients diagnosed between January 1, 1982,

and December 31, 2013, were included for the analysis. Median

age at diagnosis was 72 years and males constituted 60.9% of

patients. The baseline characteristics stratified by calendar pe-

riods are shown in Table 1. Also, shown in Table 1, is the dis-

tribution of comorbidities at CLL diagnosis where 61.9% had no

comorbidity at diagnosis, 15.5% had 1 CCI point and 22.6% had

2+ CCI points.

4.2 \| Survival and excess mortality

Comparing the calendar periods 1993–­2002 and 2003–­2013 to

1982–­1992, there was a significant decrease in mortality adjusted for

age and sex with EMRR = 0.72 (95% CI 0.66–­0.77) and EMRR = 0.53

(95% CI 0.48–­0.58), respectively (Table 2). Increasing age was as-

sociated with increasing risk of mortality with EMRR = 1.45 (95%

CI 1.20–­1.75), EMRR = 2.72 (95% CI 2.27–­3.26), and EMRR = 5.68

(95% CI 4.67–­6.92) for the age groups, 51–­65 years, 66–­80 years,

and ≥81 years of age, respectively, compared with the age group

≤50 years of age.

Comparing temporal trends in the five-­year relative survival,

the RS increased in all age categories for both sexes over the study

period (Figure 1 and Table S3). The improvement was significant in

males 51–­65 years old (RS = 0.83, 95% CI 0.81–­0.85 in 2002 and

RS = 0.75, 95% CI 0.72–­0.78 in 1992) and in males 66–­80 years old

(RS = 0.73, 95% CI 0.70–­0.75 in 2002 and RS = 0.61, 95% CI 0.57–­

0.64 in 1992). Furthermore, the improvement was significant in fe-

males 66–­80 years old (RS = 0.80, 95% CI 0.77–­0.83 in 2002 and

RS = 0.73, 95% CI 0.69–­0.76 in 1992).

4.3 \| Cause of death analysis and the prognostic role of comorbidities

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia was the primary cause of death

in just above 40% of the total number of deaths observed in the

cohort (Table 3). CLL-­mortality decreased significantly over calen-

dar time (HR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.75–­0.81 as primary cause of death,

and HR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.74–­0.79 as primary or secondary cause of

death). The same pattern was seen for other hematological malig-

nancies (HR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.55–­0.65), infections (HR = 0.62, 95%

CI 0.52–­0.73 and HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.75–­0.83 as primary or second-

ary cause of death), cardiovascular disease (HR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.63–­

0.71), whereas for other cancers and respiratory diseases it was not.

Stratified by age groups, there was a decrease in the five-­year

CLL-­specific cumulative probability of death in patients younger

than 50 years of age in the late 1990s (Figure 2 and Figure S1). In

the age groups 51–­65 and 66–­80 years of age, the decrease was

more continuous throughout the study period. In patients older than

81 years at diagnosis, there was a rise in the cumulative CLL-­related

death until the mid-­1990s where the risk decreases again.

For patients with a CCI score of 0, 1, and 2+, the proportion of

CLL-­related deaths (defined from death certificate with CLL as pri-

mary or secondary cause of death) to total number of deaths was

71.2%, 69.2%, and 62.5%, respectively (Table 4). In an adjusted

model, the all-­cause, CLL-­related, and CLL-­unrelated mortality rate

increased with increasing CCI score. Compared to the CLL-­related

mortality rate in patients with 0 CCI points at CLL diagnosis, patients

with 1 and 2+ CCI points had HR = 1.35 (95% CI 1.25–­1.45) and

HR = 1.47 (95% CI: 1.37–­1.57), respectively. For CLL-­unrelated mor-

tality, the corresponding estimates were HR = 1.45 (95% CI 1.30–­

1.63) and HR = 2.09 (95% CI 1.90–­2.30).

For specific comorbidities (Table S4), the risk of CLL-­related mor-

tality was highest for dementia (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.55–­2.79), kidney

disease (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.49–­2.58), and congestive heart failure

(OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.65–­2.01).

5 \| DISCUSSION

In this large population-­based study, including more than 13,000

CLL patients diagnosed in Sweden, survival continued to improve

in the era of chemoimmunotherapy. Importantly, CLL-­related deaths

decreased over time and the rate of infection-­related mortality de-

creased, despite the increased risk of infections associated with

chemoimmunotherapy. Our results underline that the improvements

in CLL therapy introduced at the beginning of the century have re-

sulted in better survival.

We found a steady improvement in survival in CLL patients

aged 51 years and older over the study period. A slightly de-

creased survival was observed in the youngest CLL patients early

in the study period; however, the survival improved after 2002.

These trends are likely to reflect the chemotherapy introduced in

the 1990s and the chemoimmunotherapy introduced in the first

decade of the century. The increasing survival in elderly patients

early in the study period was mainly explained by decreased risk of

death not related to CLL. The improvement observed later in the

study period, however, might, in part, represent the introduction

of treatments such as dose reduced FCR and bendamustine.44,45

Apart from changed treatment regimens in CLL, other changes in

management have also occurred, such as changes in staging sys-

tems,46 management of infections, and increased specialization.47

Our findings of improved survival in this population-­based cohort

support earlier observations from clinical trials of a clinical benefit

of fludarabine-­based therapies.5–­9

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia was the primary cause of death

for more than 40% of the observed deaths, and diseases related to

CLL (other malignancies and infections) accounted for an additional

~24% of the deaths. These findings are in line with earlier findings

reported from a single-­center cohort.28 Our results emphasize the

large burden of CLL even though it is disease of elderly patients with

comorbidities12 where a large part will not require treatment early in

the disease.29–­31 The CLL-­related mortality rate decreased over cal-

endar time which underscores the role of more effective treatment in

the improved survival in recent decades. Infections and hematolog-

ical diseases decreased as a cause of death, and non-­hematological

cancers were relatively stable over time. This is important, as a rise

in these causes of death could have been expected with the intro-

duction of chemotherapy and chemoimmunotherapy. The temporal

changes were largely in accordance with a recent Danish population-­

based study, although it did not depict decreased risk over time from

infections25 as observed in the current study. Our results from the

cause of death analysis establish that the improved survival in recent

decades is largely due to decreased risk of CLL-­related mortality.

Comorbidities at CLL diagnosis were significantly associated

with increased risk of CLL-­related, CLL-­unrelated, and all-­cause

mortality. Previous studies have been conflicting regarding the

prognostic value of comorbidities in CLL.12,28,32 In the era of che-

motherapy, many patients were not considered for standard therapy

due to comorbid conditions. The association of a higher CCI score

with an increased risk of CLL-­related mortality is in contrast with

a previous single-­center study where no association was found ad-

justed for age, sex, and CLL stage at diagnosis.28 In a supplementary

analysis, we estimated the risk of CLL-­related mortality in relation

to specific comorbidities, where a high risk was associated with de-

mentia, kidney disease, and heart failure. This finding supplements

the results from a recent Danish study48 that assessed the impact of

comorbidities in CLL patients. Our results underline the importance

of considering effective treatment in CLL patients despite known

comorbidities, and fortunately, these approaches have improved

with the new targeted therapy.49

The main strength of the study is the generalizability of the real-­

world data due to its population-­based setting in Sweden, where

there is a general access to standardized up-­to-­date medical care.

The data were prospectively gathered in high-­quality nationwide

registers33,50,51 over a long study period where there was negligi-

ble loss to follow-­up. Robust statistical analysis was performed to

is directly related to CLL, it was important to also evaluate changes

with relative survival. This study has some limitations. Lead-­time

bias is of concern, as we did not have information on CLL staging

at diagnosis. Nonetheless, there was no evidence that reporting of

early-­stage CLL had been increasing according to a validation study

of the Swedish Cancer Registry.33 Another limitation is the 88%

completeness of CLL in the SCR and the diagnostic accuracy around

84%, which is lower than for many other lymphoproliferative dis-

eases.33 The revised diagnosis was typically another lymphoprolif-

erative disease, of which well-­differentiated lymphocytic lymphoma

was the most common. Importantly, underreporting did not seem

to increase with calendar periods. A limitation to the CCI analysis

is that although the inpatient register has good coverage during the

study period, the outpatient register only reaches back to 2001.

Nonetheless, this is unlikely to have a large effect as we were not

estimating temporal trends in this analysis. Finally, the end follow-­up

was 1st of January 2014 which limits the interpretation for the pa-

tients diagnosed late in the study period.

To conclude, we have in this large population-­based study shown

improvement in survival of CLL patients over the last decade, which

was largely due to improved CLL-­specific survival. In the next de-

cade, more population-­based studies are needed to evaluate the

effects of targeted therapy on survival and causes of death in real-­

world patients.
