Background: Light chain (AL) amyloidosis is a rare disease caused by the misfolding of immunoglobulin light chains produced by a plasma cell dyscrasia. Patients can present with involvement of many organs, including the heart, kidneys, liver, nerves, and other sites, resulting in a high symptom burden. Timely and accurate diagnosis is important to prevent organ failure and improve patient survival.

Objective: These evidence-based guidelines of the American Society of Hematology (ASH) are intended to support patients and clinicians in the timely and effective diagnosis of AL amyloidosis.

Methods: ASH formed a multidisciplinary guideline panel that included 22 individuals representing various medical specialties (academic and community hematology, nephrology, neurology, cardiology, internal medicine, and pathology) as well as 1 patient representative. The group was balanced to minimize potential bias from conflicts of interest. The University of Kansas Medical Center supported the guideline development process, including updating or performing systematic evidence reviews (up to 6 March 2023). The panel prioritized clinical questions and outcomes according to their importance for clinicians and patients. The panel used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, including GRADE evidence-to-decision frameworks, to assess evidence and make recommendations, which were subject to public comment.

Results: The panel agreed on 12 recommendations.

Conclusions: The use of serum immunofixation, urine immunofixation, and serum free light chains enhances the clinical suspicion of AL amyloidosis. The diagnosis of AL amyloidosis can be made effectively through surrogate biopsies, which require both a bone marrow biopsy and fat-pad sampling; however, target organ biopsies may be favored in certain clinical situations.

Summary of recommendations

These guidelines are based on original systematic reviews of evidence conducted under the direction of the Evidence-Based Practice and Impact Center at the University of Kansas Health System. The panel followed best practice for guideline development recommended by the Institute of Medicine and the Guidelines International Network (GIN). 1-3 The panel used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach 4-8 to assess the certainty in the evidence and formulate recommendations.

Light chain (AL) amyloidosis is a rare plasma cell dyscrasia that remains challenging to diagnose. Patients have high symptom burden and poor quality of life. It is common for AL amyloidosis to involve multiple organ systems. The goals of treatment are to prevent organ failure and improve quality of life and survival for patients. Diagnostic guidelines are essential to help clinicians make timely and effective diagnoses. The American Society of Hematology (ASH) undertook evidence-based guidelines for AL amyloidosis with a focus on enhancing clinical suspicion, making a diagnosis, and defining organ involvement.

Interpretation of good practice statements

As described by the GRADE guidance group, good practice statements endorse interventions or practices that the guideline panel agreed have unequivocal net benefit yet may not be widely recognized or used. 9 Good practice statements in these guidelines are not based on a systematic review of available evidence. Nevertheless, they may be interpreted as strong recommendations.

Diagnosis

RECOMMENDATION 5. For individuals with a suspicion of AL amyloidosis, the ASH guideline panel recommends against the use of bone scintigraphy (pyrophosphate \[PYP\], 3,3-diphosphono-1, 2-propanodicarboxylic acid \[DPD\], hydroxymethylene diphosphonate \[HMDP\]) for the diagnosis of AL cardiac amyloidosis (strong recommendation based on moderate certainty in the evidence about effects ⨁⨁⨁ ◯ ).

Remark:

• Clinical suspicion for AL cardiac amyloidosis is increased in

individuals with positive biomarkers (NT-proBNP, BNP, hsTroponin), imaging (ECHO/CMR compatible with cardiac amyloidosis), and immunological assays (sFLC, SIFE/UIFE) indicating the presence of a monoclonal gammopathy.

RECOMMENDATION 6. For patients without evidence of a plasma cell disorder (normal sFLC assay and no monoclonal proteins on SIFE and UIFE) and suspicion of cardiac amyloidosis, the ASH guideline panel recommends the use of bone scintigraphy (PYP, DPD, HMDP) for the diagnosis of cardiac transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis (ATTR) (strong recommendation based on moderate certainty in the evidence about effects ⨁⨁⨁ ◯ ).

Remarks:

• In order to improve diagnostic accuracy, bone scintigraphy

should be performed with single-photon-emission tomography (SPECT) rather than planar-only imaging. SPECT is needed to verify that the tracer uptake is in the myocardium and not limited to the blood pool. In patients with suspected or confirmed ATTR amyloidosis, family history needs to be assessed. However, before or once diagnosis is confirmed, genetic counseling should be offered regardless of family history since family history is frequently missing in late onset hereditary ATTR amyloidosis.

RECOMMENDATION 7. For individuals with suspected AL cardiac amyloidosis with abnormal cardiac biomarkers, diagnostic echocardiogram, and positivity in any of the following studies: SIFE, UIFE, or sFLC, the ASH guideline panel suggests either starting with performing both fat-pad sampling and bone marrow (BM) biopsy or with endomyocardial biopsy (conditional recommendation based on low certainty in the evidence about effects ⨁⨁ ◯◯ ).

• The panel recognizes that a BM biopsy should not be performed

in isolation, without immunological assays and cardiac biomarker testing. obtain diagnostic tissue biopsy with a validated subtyping method, whether through fat-pad sampling or endomyocardial biopsy. • In certain cases, endomyocardial biopsy may be favored over

Organ involvement

RECOMMENDATION 12. For individuals with proven AL amyloidosis and no cardiac symptoms, the ASH guideline panel recommends performing cardiac biomarker testing (hsTroponin and BNP or NT-proBNP) and cardiac imaging rather than not performing these tests to define the presence and extent of cardiac involvement at diagnosis (strong recommendation based on low certainty in the evidence about effects ⨁⨁ ◯◯ ).

Remarks:

• The ASH panel recognizes that “cardiac involvement” has his-

torically been defined by ECHO 10 ; however, CMR affords higher diagnostic accuracy with better performance, albeit often limited by cost and availability. • ECHO should be performed with measurement of left ventricular

GLS to evaluate for reduction in longitudinal shortening and sparing of the apex (sensitivity, 72%; may be absent in 28% of patients). • The panel recognizes that NT-proBNP has been studied more

than BNP in AL amyloidosis, but if the former is not available, BNP is an acceptable alternative cardiac biomarker. • The panel recognizes that cardiac biomarker (troponin, BNP,

NT-proBNP) results may be affected by kidney function and preexisting cardiac conditions. • The panel recognizes that most evidence in amyloidosis used

hsTroponin T, but recognizes that some centers may not have access to this test, and other troponin measures that are less extensively evaluated in AL amyloidosis can be used. • The panel recognizes that an hsTroponin T cutoff of 35 ng/L

Values and preferences

Values and preferences varied depending on the stage of the diagnostic pathway. For tests that enhanced clinical suspicion, particularly initial tests, greater emphasis was placed on the sensitivity of the test to minimize the number of patients with AL amyloidosis being missed. In these cases, specificity was valued less because subsequent tests would follow. For diagnostic tests, higher value was placed on specificity to prevent patients from receiving unnecessary treatment for conditions they do not have. Timeliness and avoiding delays in diagnosis were considered at every step of the diagnostic pathway.

Explanations and other considerations

The panel used the PICO (P = population, patient, or problem; I = intervention, exposure, or prognostic factor; C = comparison or control; O = outcome, which is what you want to measure, improve, or affect) model to structure its clinical questions. During PICO development, the panel prioritized questions that encom-passed the diagnostic pathway of patients with AL amyloidosis, from “red flag” signs and symptoms, to organ involvement. The panel also discussed the numerous surrogate site biopsy options available for clinicians (Table 1). The panel decided to focus on abdominal fat-pad samples and BM biopsies because they are the most used. Target organs in systemic amyloidosis were defined as those that are symptomatic, and the underlying condition gener-ated a red flag whereas surrogate biopsy sites are those that, despite frequently being involved by amyloidosis, are typically asymptomatic and easily accessible. Thus, the surrogate biopsy serves as a less invasive substitute for a target organ biopsy. Target organs include the heart, kidneys, liver, and peripheral nerves, whereas GI tract luminal involvement may be considered a target or surrogate site, depending on the severity of involvement. Rectal/GI biopsies were considered a surrogate site if patients did not present with GI symptoms. In amyloidosis, red flags are signs and symptoms that herald underlying amyloidosis and thereby may aid in early detection of amyloidosis. 12,13

### Introduction

Aim of these guidelines and specific objectives

The purpose of these guidelines is to provide evidence-based recommendations for the diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. The primary goals of these guidelines are to review, critically appraise, and implement evidence-based recommendations that will enhance early detection, timeliness, and accuracy of diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. Through improved provider and patient education of the available evidence and creation of evidence-based recommendations, these guidelines aim to provide clinical decision support that will result in clear diagnostic decision-making with known potential outcomes and enable timely diagnosis of AL amyloidosis by multidisciplinary teams.

The target audience includes patients, community and academic hematologists, general practitioners, internists, nephrologists, cardiologists, neurologists, pathologists, and other clinicians, as well as decision-makers. Policymakers interested in these guidelines include those involved in developing local, national, or international plans, with the goal to accurately diagnose AL amyloidosis in the shortest timeline. This document may also serve as the basis for adaptation by local, regional, or national guideline panels.

Description of the health problem

The amyloidoses are a group of rare disorders caused by protein misfolding, leading to a complex pathway of deposition of insoluble abnormal amyloid fibrils in various organs. 23 This accumulation can lead to an altered function of the tissue involved. The term “amyloid” was noted in the 19th century, with disease classification systems starting in the 1970s, at which time the terms such as “primary amyloidosis,” “secondary amyloidosis,” and “hereditary amyloidosis” began to be adopted. Amyloidosis syndromes are now much better understood, and the International Society of Amyloidosis has standardized the naming convention for amyloidosis. 24 Following “A,” which stands for “amyloidosis,” current nomenclature is based on the protein deposited in the amyloid fibrils, that is, AL for light chain amyloidosis. There have been 42 different kinds of amyloid fibril proteins identified in humans; some are found in a systemic presentation whereas others are seen in a localized presentation. The most common types of amyloidosis are AL amyloidosis, serum amyloid A protein– associated (AA) amyloidosis, and transthyreitin (TTR) amyloidosis (ATTR). ATTR exists in wild type and variant forms. More recently, the International Consensus Classification project has suggested that primary AL amyloidosis be referred to as immunoglobulin AL amyloidosis when systemic and an “amyloid tumor” when it exists as localized AL amyloidosis. 25 For this guideline, the panel has continued with the International Society for Amyloidosis nomenclature of AL amyloidosis because this terminology is most frequently used in published literature.

AL amyloidosis is a plasma cell dyscrasia, which occurs when clonal plasma cells produce excess light chains that misfold and deposit in various organs as amyloid fibrils. AL amyloidosis is a rare disease, with an incidence of 5.1 to 12.8 cases per million person-years. Based on health insurance claims data, the prevalence of

amyloidosis is 40.5 cases per million; this represents an increasing prevalence over time. 11,26 Amyloid deposits can be found in various organs but most commonly in the heart, kidneys, GI tract, and peripheral nerves. Symptoms can be nonspecific such as fatigue and weight loss, or can be organ-related symptoms such as shortness of breath, edema, and sensory changes. This condition can be associated with significant morbidity and mortality, especially with late diagnoses.

For AL amyloidosis, the diagnosis is confirmed by tissue analysis. Patients are then staged, and risk stratified through imaging and laboratory investigations. Together this helps to prognosticate using various AL staging classification systems. After diagnosis and staging, the aim is to initiate therapy as soon as possible. First-line treatment includes agents that target the plasma cell clone (proteasome inhibitors with monoclonal antibodies, ie, D-VCD \[daratumumab, bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, and dexametha-sone\], and, in some cases, high-dose chemotherapy with autolo-gous hematopoietic cell transplantation). There are several practice guidelines highlighting strategies for treatment of AL amyloidosis, including the need for supportive care. 27-29 Clinical outcomes have improved over the decades but early death (\<6 months) in patients with high-risk cardiac amyloidosis still represents a major challenge. 30,31 Retrospective studies demonstrate that overall survival is \>43% at 5 years after diagnosis and drops to 27% at 10 years after diagnosis. 32

Delays in diagnosis, often spanning months to \>2 years, are common in AL amyloidosis. 33-36 Such delays in the diagnostic journey can relate to complexity of symptoms and overlap with many other conditions. Often the diagnosis of AL amyloidosis is not considered. Patients are frequently seen by many health care providers of varying specialties before a diagnosis is made. Patient outcomes are affected negatively by diagnostic delays, particularly in patients with cardiac involvement. 37 This highlights the need to increase awareness of symptoms associated with AL amyloidosis and for clear diagnostic pathways for this disease.

The ASH has recognized the need for evidence-based guidelines for a diagnostic pathway to facilitate timely and accurate diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. There are data showing a benefit of such guidelines in rare diseases to help hematologists and other specialists who encounter such patients infrequently. 38,39 The panel first defined the optimal diagnostic pathway of relevance for AL amyloidosis including (1) enhancing clinical suspicion, (2) diagnosis, and (3) organ involvement (Figure 1). Also, “red flag” signs and symptoms, which would increase the suspicion of AL amyloidosis were defined to enhance awareness and entry into a diagnostic pathway. 12,40 The panel explored relevant questions for systematic review, which were distributed throughout the phases of this pathway. Because the diagnosis typically depends on multidisciplinary input with a recognition that the hematologist may not be the first person to see such patients, the panel consisted of a wide variety of medical specialists. The panel also recognized that models of care may vary from centralized systems at academic centers to decentralized systems in community practice. Recommendations were considered with these nuances in mind. At the outset, the panel developed recommendations that are more relevant to high-income countries in the hope that these recommendations can then be adapted by organizations and clinicians in more resource-constrained environments.

### Methods

The guideline panel developed and graded the recommendations and assessed the certainty of the supporting evidence following the GRADE approach. 4,7,41-43 The overall guideline development process, including funding of the work, panel formation, management of conflicts of interest, internal and external review, and organizational approval, was guided by ASH policies and procedures derived from the GIN–McMaster guideline development checklist 44,45 and was intended to meet recommendations for trustworthy guidelines by the Institute of Medicine and the GIN. 1-3,46 When limited or no published evidence was available, an expert evidence survey was used to collect the panel members’ unpublished observations. 46

How to use these guidelines

ASH guidelines are primarily intended to help clinicians make decisions about diagnostic and treatment alternatives. Other purposes are to inform policy, education, and advocacy, and to state future research needs. They may also be used by patients. These guidelines are not intended to serve or be construed as a standard of care. Clinicians must make decisions based on the clinical presentation of each individual patient, ideally through a shared process that considers the patient’s values and preferences with respect to the anticipated outcomes of the chosen option. Decisions may be constrained by the realities of a specific clinical setting and local resources including, but not limited to, institutional policies, time limitations, or availability of treatments. These guidelines may not include all appropriate methods of care for the clinical scenarios described. As science advances and new evidence becomes available, recommendations may become outdated. Following these guidelines cannot guarantee successful outcomes. The ASH does not warrant or guarantee any products described in these guidelines.

Statements about the underlying values and preferences as well as qualifying remarks accompanying each recommendation are its integral parts and serve to facilitate more accurate interpretation. Recommendation 1

Good practice statement

Serum and urine protein electrophoresis should not be the screening tests or only tests for suspected or confirmed AL amyloidosis. A combination of electrophoresis and immunofixation on serum and urine along with serum immunoglobulin FLC testing is required.

Summary of the evidence. The ASH guideline panel found no systematic reviews that addressed this question. The panel identified 5 cohort and case-control studies published after the search for the systematic review was completed. These studies measured sensitivity and specificity of SIFE, UIFE, and sFLC assay to increase suspicion of AL amyloidosis in patients suspected of having cardiac amyloidosis. The panel judged the diagnostic performance of these tests to be very accurate because these tests have high sensitivity (99%) and, hence, are an optimal screening tool for increasing suspicion of AL amyloidosis. The specificity (77%) of these tests is low, especially when considering the general population. However, it is appropriate for differentiating AL and ATTR cardiac amyloidosis in suspected cardiac amyloidosis population. All studies demonstrate the specificity of SIFE/UIFE/ sFLC in a specific clinical scenario (AL vs non-AL amyloidosis). The specificity in that clinical scenario may not necessarily reflect the comparison addressed in this question. Therefore, the specificity will not play a major role in decision-making and, hence, is unlikely to affect the recommendation. These studies did not include the duration between the index and reference tests and the same reference test was not used in all the studies.

Benefits. The guideline panel judged that the desirable effects of performing SIFE/UIFE and sFLC assay is large to very large. Performing SIFE, UIFE, and sFLC testing in suspected cardiac amyloidosis offers numerous benefits, including high diagnostic sensitivity/specificity, early detection, and a decrease in the time interval between establishment of diagnosis and treatment initiation. Early and accurate diagnosis can decrease mortality. 55 Although false positives (detection of monoclonal protein and not having AL amyloidosis) can be a limitation of these tests, it is important for health care providers to carefully consider the risks and benefits of testing, as well as to communicate effectively with patients about the possibility of monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance and the need for further evaluation if necessary. 56

Harms and burden. Although IFE and sFLC assay are essential diagnostic tools in the evaluation of suspected cardiac

amyloidosis, they do carry small potential harms and burdens. Physical discomfort of a venipuncture and financial costs are minimal and may not be adequately and uniformly covered by insurance. 57 False positives can lead to unnecessary anxiety and further invasive testing, whereas false negatives may delay appropriate diagnosis and treatment. 58 However, given the available evidence, the guideline panel considered the risk of adverse effects most likely to be small. 59

Other EtD criteria and considerations. The panel reviewed the certainty of evidence regarding the management effects as high because SIFE/UIFE and sFLC testing directly inform the next steps in managing suspected cardiac amyloidosis. If these tests return positive, the patient would be referred to hematology and/or cardiology for further evaluation. The panel also concluded that there is probably no significant uncertainty or variability in how much key stakeholders value the outcomes of SIFE/UIFE and sFLC testing for cardiac amyloidosis. Patients suspected of having cardiac amyloidosis are often patients with heart failure seeking a definitive diagnosis for their symptoms. Therefore, pursuing a diagnostic pathway that provides timely positive or negative results holds significant value for these patients.

Regarding the resources required to conduct these tests, the panel acknowledged that the cost and availability of these tests vary by country. However, they believe that in high-income settings, these tests are readily available and relatively low-cost. Although there is no direct research evidence to support this, the panel determined that implementing SIFE/UIFE and sFLC testing would probably enhance health equity. This is because Black patients with conditions such as ATTR amyloidosis, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), or myeloma are likely to be identified through clinical suspicion, ensuring they receive appropriate diagnostic testing and follow-up care. Evidence profiles with the characteristics of all included studies and the complete EtD framework are online for recommendation 1.

Conclusions and research needs for this recommendation. The guideline panel determined that there is a high certainty of evidence for a net health benefit from performing SIFE/UIFE and sFLC assay in patients suspected of having cardiac amyloidosis. Based on the body of available evidence, it is likely that these tests reduce the risk of delay in diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. There is very low certainty that there is an effect of performing these tests on other outcomes. However, because of low certainty of evidence or no published information about other outcomes, the fact that the panel did not find the evidence of an effect on these outcomes does not imply that such an effect does not exist.

Discussion. Monoclonal sFLC of each individual patient is unique, which leaves sFLC quantification subject to analytical issues such as inconsistency in linear responses, imprecision, or bias due to reagent lot-to-lot variation, with a clear drift noted toward higher sFLC ratios over the years. Consequently, in 2022, laboratories worldwide were no longer able to reproduce the defined sFLC ranges in healthy controls as they were published in the seminal paper in 2002. Laboratories may need to update their practices and reference ranges to reflect changes in sFLC distribution and ensure the validity and reliability of sFLC testing.

N-latex assays on Siemens’ BNII nephelometric analyzer and assays from The Binding Site are 2 common approaches for the measurement of sFLC in clinical practice. Summary of the evidence. CMR can be helpful to evaluate the etiology of a cardiomyopathy by looking at patterns in fibrosis detected by late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) or changes in extracellular volume (ECV). Systematic review yielded 7 cohort and case-control studies that examined the diagnostic test accuracy of the finding of LGE on CMR for diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis. These studies reported the accuracy of described LGE patterns consistent with cardiac amyloidosis; there were 2 additional studies that reported presence of LGE without specifying the pattern nor distribution, and these studies were excluded. However, no studies that address the diagnostic test accuracy of ECV using endomyocardial biopsy as a reference were identified. The panel found the diagnostic test accuracy of CMR to be between accurate and very accurate, with a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 87%. 71-73

Benefits. In patients with abnormal monoclonal studies and abnormal cardiac biomarkers, ECHO is often the initial diagnostic test to evaluate the possibility of cardiac amyloidosis. When the echocardiogram is diagnostic for cardiac amyloidosis, CMR may offer limited additional diagnostic benefit, and it may delay care, because these cases warrant expeditious tissue biopsy for disease confirmation and initiation of treatment. However, in patients who have nondiagnostic findings on ECHO, CMR offers significant benefit in its ability to assist with risk stratification, because in patients with negative scans, cardiac amyloidosis can be reliably ruled out with 87% specificity, whereas cardiac amyloidosis with can be detected in patients with 95% sensitivity. The high sensitivity of CMR will increase

our clinical ability to discriminate disease, pursue tissue biopsy, and initiate treatment. The desirable effect of having negative CMR results is reliably ruling out cardiac amyloidosis and, thus, avoiding unnecessary invasive tissue biopsy and any associated complications. For these reasons, the desirable effects of performing CMR in patients with nondiagnostic ECHO are deemed large.

Harms and burden. Undesirable effects are very low in routine CMR procedures. In fact, across the studies evaluated, major complications were not reported, and the incidence of minor complications such as nausea, vomiting, and palpitations was reported at 0.04% to 0.06%. 71 The indirect undesirable effects of performing CMR relate to increased rates of false positives resulting in unnecessary biopsies, or false negatives reducing suspicion of disease and delaying diagnosis in patients who truly have the disease. It is also worth mentioning that there may be rare cases of positive CMR results, whereas the endomyocardial biopsy misses the diagnosis, particularly in cases of patchy LGE on CMR; these cases would require additional investigation.

Other EtD criteria and considerations. The panel recognizes that CMR is a resource with moderate associated costs for most patients in well-resourced health care settings. Furthermore, the panel discussed the probability of our recommendation reducing health equity, because the test is expensive and not as widely available as cardiac ultrasound. Availability of the needed equipment, technical knowledge of imaging protocols, and physician expertise for interpretation may be limited in rural areas as well as developing countries. Nonetheless, the balance of effects favors performing CMR in patients with nondiagnostic ECHO findings in settings in which it is readily available or in locations in which these barriers can be overcome. The panel determined CMR to be definitely or probably acceptable to most patients. Apart from its superior ability to aid in the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis, it is the only imaging modality known to be capable of disease quantification. Disease burden may be quantified by ECV measurements, which may be tracked for disease progression, and potentially, for response to medical intervention. Recommendation 5

For individuals with a suspicion of AL amyloidosis, the ASH guideline panel recommends against the use of bone scintigraphy (PYP, DPD, HMDP) for the diagnosis of AL cardiac amyloidosis (strong recommendation based on moderate certainty in the evidence about effects ⨁⨁⨁ ◯ ).

Remark: • Clinical suspicion for cardiac AL amyloidosis is increased

in individuals with positive biomarkers (NT-proBNP, BNP, hsTroponin), imaging (ECHO/CMR compatible with cardiac amyloidosis), and immunological assays (sFLC, SIFE/UIFE), indicating the presence of a monoclonal gammopathy.

Summary of the evidence. The 99m Tc tracers PYP, DPD, and HMDP that were initially used to evaluate bone may also be taken up by the heart if there is amyloid deposition. There commonly is differential tracer uptake in the heart depending on the type of amyloidosis. In patients with cardiac AL amyloidosis there is typically no or limited tracer uptake whereas in cardiac ATTR amyloidosis there may be more pronounced uptake. Bone scintigraphy should be performed with SPECT and not with planar imaging only to confirm that tracer uptake is in the myocardium and avoid false positives. 74 The tracer uptake in the heart is visually compared with bone or the heart uptake is compared with the contralateral lung to generate a heart-to– contralateral lung (H:CL) ratio. Patients with AL amyloidosis and cardiac involvement may have no myocardial uptake (visual score of 0), myocardial uptake less than bone (visual score of 1), or grade 2 uptake (myocardial uptake equal to bone) with a H:CL ratio of \<1.5 whereas patients with cardiac ATTR amyloidosis often have grade 2 (myocardial uptake equal to bone) or grade 3 uptake (myocardial uptake greater than bone) that is confirmed on SPECT and a H:CL ratio of ≥1. 5. 75,76

The panel evaluated 7 studies with 869 patients to evaluate the use of visual grades 1 and 2 to diagnose AL amyloidosis. 75,77-82

The pretest probability of cardiac AL amyloidosis was estimated to be 1%. Using a visual score of 1, the sensitivity was 43% and specificity 12%. When a visual score of 2 was used to diagnose AL amyloidosis, the sensitivity was 17% and specificity 18%. There was concern for bias of the data because the studies did not specify the time between the BM biopsy and scintigraphy.

Benefits. Bone scintigraphy with SPECT is a noninvasive test that can be useful for diagnosis of cardiac involvement in some forms of cardiac amyloidosis. This test appears to be most useful for diagnosis of cardiac ATTR amyloidosis in the absence of a monoclonal protein. In patients with a monoclonal protein, bone scintigraphy with visual uptake of ≤2 is not sufficient to diagnosis cardiac AL amyloidosis because of its low sensitivity and specificity. However, visual uptake of ≤1 may be useful to rule out cardiac ATTR amyloidosis in select patients with advanced cardiac disease (Figure 3).

Harms and burden. Bone scintigraphy has a low sensitivity and specificity to diagnose AL amyloidosis using a visual score of 1 or 2 and is not accurate for diagnosing cardiac AL amyloidosis. Patients with false negatives will have a delay of diagnosis and will need to undergo additional testing to reach a diagnosis. Additionally, these patients will have been exposed to radiation and may still require a tissue biopsy. Patients with a false-positive diagnosis of cardiac AL amyloidosis may receive inappropriate treatment.

Other EtD criteria and considerations. In patients with suspected cardiac amyloidosis, it is imperative to rule out a plasma cell dyscrasia with sFLC and SIFE and UIFE before bone scintigraphy is considered. In patients with evidence of a plasma cell dyscrasia, tissue biopsy of a surrogate or target organ should not be delayed, and bone scintigraphy should not be performed because this will result in diagnostic delays. Evidence profiles with the characteristics of all included studies and the complete EtD framework are online for recommendation 5.

Conclusions and research needs for this recommendation. Bone scintigraphy has a low sensitivity and specificity to diagnose AL amyloidosis and should not be used to confirm a diagnosis of cardiac AL amyloidosis and may lead to delays in diagnosis or misdiagnosis. In certain cases, bone scintigraphy with visual uptake of ≤1 may be used to rule out cardiac ATTR amyloidosis in patients without a monoclonal protein.

Good practice statement

The ASH panel agreed that in individuals with proven amyloid deposits on histology, amyloid subtyping using a validated method must be performed in a timely fashion.

Summary of the evidence. Systemic AL amyloidosis can coexist in patients with plasma cell dyscrasias including newly diagnosed multiple myeloma and smoldering multiple myeloma; AL amyloidosis can also be detected in some patients with low clonal plasma cell burden and MGUS. BM biopsies are routinely performed for the diagnosis of multiple myeloma and smoldering myeloma. Congo Red staining on previously performed BM biopsy for the detection of amyloid presents a potential method for detecting concurrent AL amyloidosis in these patients.

There is a paucity of literature on the prevalence of amyloid deposits in BM biopsy specimens in patients with MGUS such that this population could not be evaluated by a systematic review.

The ASH guideline panel found no systematic reviews that addressed this question. The panel identified 21 relevant cohort and case-control studies. These studies measured the incidence of amyloidosis as detected by Congo Red stain of BM samples. Samples were from patients who already had BM biopsies performed as standard of care in patients with plasma cell dyscrasias (multiple myeloma, smoldering multiple myeloma). The panel judged the diagnostic performance of this testing to be accurate, with a sensitivity of 55% (95% confidence interval, 45-64). The panel would like to highlight that diagnostic test accuracy is heavily dependent on user skill; experience and expertise in the interpretation of Congo Red staining for amyloidosis diagnosis is required. In the literature, the specificity estimates described do not necessarily reflect clinical scenarios of AL amyloidosis because other subtypes of amyloidosis were included within the

disease-negative population. Therefore, the specificity did not play a role in decision-making. Another limitation in the literature is that these studies did not include the duration between the index and reference tests and the same reference test(s) were not used.

Benefits. The panel judged that the desirable effects of performing Congo Red staining on BM biopsies already performed as standard of care in patients with multiple myeloma and smoldering multiple myeloma as moderate. Performing Congo Red stain on such samples has the benefit of early diagnosis and decrease in the time interval between establishment of AL amyloidosis diagnosis and amyloid-directed therapy. Early and accurate diagnosis may also decrease mortality. In cases with false-negative testing, that is, amyloid deposits were present but not detected, these patients may have a delayed diagnosis with significant impact on prognosis. However, there is discordance regarding the impact of finding Congo Red–positive amyloid deposits on prognosis of these patients. Petruzziello et al 187 showed limited impact on prognosis whereas Vela-Ojeda et al 188 showed it as an independent high-risk prognostic factor. Nonetheless, the panel judged this as moderate for desirable effects, favoring the early detection of systemic AL amyloidosis.

Although false positives (positive Congo Red staining on BM biopsy and not having AL amyloidosis; ie, BM-only AL amyloid deposits) can be a limitation of this test, it is important for health care providers to carefully consider the risks and benefits of testing, as well as to communicate effectively with patients about the possibility of amyloidosis and the need for further evaluation if necessary. The panel would also like to highlight cases of inconclusive results from BM biopsy tests and the possibility for additional testing, which would otherwise not be required.

Harms and burden. Congo Red staining on such BM biopsy samples can increase diagnosis of amyloidosis. However, they do carry small potential harms and burdens. False positives can lead to unnecessary anxiety and further invasive testing, whereas false negatives may delay appropriate diagnosis and treatment. There is also a possible harm due to inconclusive results from BM biopsy typing. The panel does recognize the possibility for additional testing, which would otherwise not be required. In 1 study, 187 after a mean follow-up of around 2 years (range, 2-99 months), 0 of 51 patients have developed overt signs attributable to systemic amyloidosis and being observed with no plasma cell–directed therapy. In another patient cohort, 189 2 patients with asymptomatic amyloid deposits did not develop systemic amyloidosis after 7 years of follow-up. In the cohort from Chakraborty et al, 190 3 of 68 patients with positive BM developed systemic amyloidosis. Among these 3 populations, 3 of 121 (2.4%) patients with amyloid deposits developed systemic amyloidosis. However, the impact of treatment for those patients was not evaluated. According to estimates from centers of the panel members, the percentage of inconclusive results from mass spectrometry on BM biopsy is \<5%. 191 The panel believes that there is no direct harm from the test because the BM biopsy was already performed for the diagnosis of multiple myeloma/smoldering myeloma but understands the potential for possible harm due to inconclusive results from BM biopsy typing. Recommendation 12

For individuals with proven AL amyloidosis and with no cardiac symptoms, the ASH guideline panel recommends performing cardiac biomarker testing (hsTroponin, and BNP or NT-proBNP) and cardiac imaging rather than not performing these tests to define the presence and extent of cardiac involvement at diagnosis (strong recommendation based on low certainty in the evidence about effects ⨁⨁ ◯◯ ).

Summary of the evidence: ECHO. ECHO is a widely available noninvasive diagnostic test that can evaluate for structural and functional changes associated with cardiac amyloidosis. 91 The panel reviewed 36 studies with 4956 patients that evaluated echocardiographic features seen in cardiac amyloidosis. Echocardiographic features with high sensitivity and specificity were prioritized to provide incremental diagnostic information and included LVH, diastolic dysfunction, and reduced GLS with an apical sparing pattern. Although, in clinical practice, integration of multiple echocardiographic features may be useful to increase clinical suspicion for amyloidosis, the panel could not make recommendations about the possible additive benefit of combining these parameters because of the bias that occurs when evaluating different components of the same test.

Left ventricular wall thickness on ECHO is typically obtained by measuring the interventricular septum (IVS) and posterior wall (PW). 193 A left ventricular wall thickness (IVS and/or PW) of ≥12 mm unexplained by other causes may be useful to screen for cardiac involvement in patients with AL amyloidosis (IVS: sensitivity 77%, specificity 71%; and PW: sensitivity 76%-100%, specificity 84%-89%). Diastolic function is assessed by ECHO by integrating multiple features. 194 One technique to evaluate diastolic function compares the flow across the mitral valve in early diastole from early filling (E wave) with late diastole due to atrial contraction (A wave) to generate a ratio of early vs late mitral valve flow known as the E:A ratio. The E:A ratio is typically ≥2 in patients with more advanced diastolic dysfunction due to high filling pressures. An increased E:A ratio has a sensitivity of 45% to 65% and specificity of 85% to 98%. Two studies were excluded from the analysis with an E:A ratio near 1 because this may be close to normal in an older population. The E:A ratio combined with other echocardiographic parameters is used to assess for the stage of diastolic dysfunction from I to III, with higher grades indicating more severe impairment. Stage II and III diastolic dysfunction are often seen in cardiac amyloidosis and combined have a sensitivity of 71% and specificity of 78%. Grade III diastolic dysfunction, also known as a restrictive diastolic filling pattern, has a sensitivity of 42% and specificity of 89%.

GLS is a semiautomated technique that can evaluate subclinical changes in mechanical deformation of the left ventricle. 195,196

GLS values are typically negative when myocardial segments shorten, and a higher absolute value indicates more shortening/ contraction. For example, GLS of −14 is reduced or worse compared with a GLS of −17. There are no established normal ranges for GLS because they may vary with the vendor and software package; however, for reference the American Society of Echocardiography states that a value in the range of −20 would be expected in a healthy person. 182 Reduced GLS equal to or less than −17% has a high sensitivity (86%) and specificity (78%) to identify cardiac amyloidosis. In cardiac amyloidosis, the commonly observed pattern is that the strain is preserved/normal in the left ventricular apex compared with the basal and midsegments in which it is impaired and is referred to as an apical sparing pattern. 76 The reduced contractile function of the basal and midsegments leads to an overall reduction in GLS when the segments are averaged. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of an apical sparing pattern was 72% and 78%, respectively. Therefore, in

Summary of the evidence: CMR. Pooled analysis suggests the diagnostic test accuracy of CMR to be between accurate and very accurate, with a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 87%. This determination is a product of systematic review across 7 cohort and case-control studies that examined the diagnostic test accuracy of the finding of LGE on CMR for diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis.

Summary of the evidence: biomarkers. Cardiac biomarkers, NT-proBNP, BNP, and troponin, are an essential part of cardiac involvement assessment in patients with AL amyloidosis; cardiac involvement would have a significant impact on patient prognosis and management. Additionally, cardiac biomarkers also form part of the initial staging in any patients with newly diagnosed AL amyloidosis. 197,198

The ASH guideline panel analyzed 8 studies that included 645 patients, using NT-proBNP at a 332 ng/L cutoff, and the panel found that it was highly sensitive (99%) and had low specificity (53%). The panel also analyzed the data for BNP in 6 studies including 257 patients with AL amyloidosis using 3 different cutoffs and the data were heterogeneous. The panel also looked at hsTroponin at a cutoff of 35 ng/L, which showed high sensitivity (83%) and specificity (86%).

Benefits: ECHO with left ventricle GLS strain. Given that ECHO is a low-cost, widely available diagnostic modality, and the fact that cardiac involvement in patients with AL amyloidosis is an important prognostic factor used in staging the disease and guiding treatment options, ECHO in asymptomatic patients offers significant benefit and may help direct patient care.

Benefits: CMR. In comparison with ECHO, CMR offers greater levels of sensitivity and specificity for presence of cardiac amyloidosis in patients with AL amyloidosis who may not have cardiac symptoms. As mentioned, cardiac involvement affects treatment choice and prognosis and, thus, ascertaining presence or absence of amyloid within the myocardium is an important step in management of this systemic disease. Patients with negative scans, cardiac amyloidosis can be reliably ruled out with 87% specificity, whereas cardiac amyloidosis can be detected with 95% sensitivity. Apart from disease discrimination, CMR offers the added benefit of disease quantification with measurement of ECV. Quantification of disease burden may assist with greater levels of risk stratification, assessment of disease progression, and moni-toring disease response to various therapeutic strategies.

Benefits: biomarkers. The panel found that there is a moderate desirable effect of performing cardiac biomarker testing (NT-proBNP, BNP, and troponin), 199 found that NT-proBNP was cost-effective in patients that were assessed for heart failure. The panel believes the same could be considered in patients with AL amyloidosis due to its sensitivity; biomarkers can be reported in minutes, making them feasible; and all these factors favor the balance the effects of the intervention.

Harms and burden: ECHO. ECHO is a safe a noninvasive test with no major adverse events. Minor side effects include possible patient discomfort and mild bruising. The price of ECHO varies by country, health system, and the type of medical insurance. The panel found the cost varies from US $204 to $3000 in the United States, and is €88 in the United Kingdom. The costs may be affected by the presence or absence of medical insurance and the location where the test is performed. 199,200 There is no research evidence about the cost-effectiveness of performing ECHO in patients with AL amyloidosis. The panel did not think it was appropriate to extrapolate cost-effectiveness data from other populations.

The panel recommends that GLS be performed to evaluate for cardiac involvement in patients with AL amyloidosis. The technique is semiautomated and requires the use of special analysis software to existing ECHO machines. Although additional software may be required, the panel thought this would be feasible to perform during standard ECHO to improve detection of cardiac involvement in patients with amyloidosis.

The parameters described to screen for cardiac involvement by ECHO may be less sensitive in early cardiac disease and could lead to false-negative diagnoses. In patients with early cardiac involvement with a nondiagnostic echocardiogram, CMR may be more sensitive to detect cardiac involvement if available and not cost-prohibitive.

Harms and burden: CMR. Risk of harm from CMR is very low. Across the studies evaluated, major complications were not reported, and the incidence of minor complications such as nausea, vomiting, and palpitations was reported at 0.04% to 0.06%.

Harms and burden: biomarkers. There are no direct undesirable effects related to cardiac biomarkers because the test is safe and simple, and part of routine blood testing. The panel concluded that the undesirable effects of performing biomarker testing would be small.

The panel recognizes that institutional level of experience is needed because some centers may not have access or routinely perform hsTroponin analysis, and there are several manufacturers, which can lead to variability in the results. The panel recognizes that BNP may be more readily available in most centers and is an alternative to NT-proBNP.

Limitations of these guidelines

In the realm of rare diseases, evidence generation is limited by difficulty in performing properly sized studies and limitations in dissemination of results, which are often heterogeneous. An inherent limitation in the evidence informing the recommendations is the rarity of AL amyloidosis leading to limited evidence. Despite this, use of the limited evidence in conjunction with the panel’s experience in diagnosis of AL amyloidosis in an open discussion within the prioritized clinical scenarios, allowed for the construc-tion of detailed recommendations. This allowed for the discussion regarding the impact of different health care resources and geographic variations in the EtD considerations. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that, due to the complexity of the diagnostic consideration of AL amyloidosis, the single comparative nature of the PICO questions may not fully capture diagnostic nuances or contextual factors.

The limitations of these guidelines are inherent in the low or very low certainty in the evidence that the panel identified for many of the questions. Low and very low certainty evidence was mainly due to risk of bias in the included studies as well as imprecision. Considering the different references and different cutoffs for some of the evidence used to address certain PICO questions, the panel relied on the face validity of the studies for what a normal or abnormal test is. The availability of diagnostic tests and the expertise to interpret the results are variable and may affect how these guidelines will be used.
