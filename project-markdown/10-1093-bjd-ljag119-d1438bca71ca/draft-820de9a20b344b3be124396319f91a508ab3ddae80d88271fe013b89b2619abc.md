Dear Editor, Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is the precursor condition for multiple myeloma and other haematological malignancies, which are associated with increased risks of secondary malignancies, including skin cancer.1 However, whether MGUS itself predisposes individuals to skin cancer has not been studied in large population-based cohorts. MGUS is an asymptomatic premalignant plasma cell disorder that does not require treatment but is monitored due to its potential progression to multiple myeloma or related disorders. Prior studies have included cohorts of MGUS diagnosed during follow-up for other medical problems leading to significant selection bias.2 To our knowledge, this is the first study exploring the association between MGUS and skin cancer risk in a large cohort, using a nationwide cancer registry with virtually no loss of follow-up. We speculated that a relationship between MGUS and skin cancer might occur on the basis that: (i) MGUS is a precursor to multiple myeloma, a known skin cancer risk factor;1 and (ii) MGUS has been associated with impaired humoral and cellular immunity,3 a key risk factor for skin cancer development.1

We investigated this association using data from the iStopMM study and the Icelandic Cancer Registry (ICR), encompassing a cohort of 75 419 individuals, 3666 of whom were diagnosed with MGUS.4–6 Briefly, the iStopMM study is the largest population-based MGUS screening study ever conducted. It was designed to evaluate the benefits and harms of screening for MGUS.4,7 Briefly, the entirety of the eligible Icelandic population, 148 711 individuals, were contacted to voluntarily enrol. Once enrolled, study participants underwent peripheral blood collection sampling to screen for M protein using capillary zone electrophoresis and serum analysis for free immunoglobulin light chains (FLC). In total 75 419 participants were screened for MGUS. Individuals with abnormal M protein or FLC results were randomized into three study arms. Participants in arm 1 were not informed of their MGUS status; arm 2 was followed up according to current

guidelines; and arm 3 had more intensive clinical follow-up. Full details related to the original iStopMM study design have been published elsewhere.4

Skin cancers were classified as basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), SCC in situ (SCCis), melanoma, or melanoma in situ (MIS). The ICR was used to identify any skin cancers occurring in the 80 759 participants by linking each participant’s unique identification number. The ICR is a high-quality registry, containing complete case records of all pathologically confirmed skin cancers in Iceland since 1981.5,6 A detailed description of the ICR is provided elsewhere.5 Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were employed to compare skin cancer frequency and survival between MGUS and non-MGUS groups, adjusting for age and sex. Sensitivity analyses evaluated subgroups based on timing of cancer diagnosis relative to MGUS diagnosis, MGUS isotype, and progression to smouldering myeloma. Individuals were randomized into three separate groups depending on the nature of follow-up visits and awareness of MGUS diagnosis.

In adjusted analyses, MGUS was not associated with increased frequency of BCC \[odds ratio (OR) 1.07, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.93–1.21; P = 0.35\], SCC (OR 1.07, 95% CI 0.88–1.30; P = 0.51), SCCis (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.76–1.24; P = 0.83), MIS (OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.80–1.96; P = 0.33) or melanoma (OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.80–1.47; P = 0.60) (Table 1). Survival analysis revealed no significant differences in outcomes between MGUS and non-MGUS groups for any skin cancer type. Sensitivity analysis did not show a difference when all skin cancers were combined in one analysis, nor in skin cancer frequency in those who progressed to smouldering myeloma, nor in those who had more frequent follow-up or were aware of their MGUS diagnosis using the different follow-up arms.

These findings suggest that MGUS does not significantly impact skin cancer risk or survival. Our study’s strengths include its population-based design where more than half of the eligible Icelandic population was recruited, and the use of the nationwide ICR, ensuring complete case ascertainment and minimizing selection bias. Nonetheless, limitations such as the relatively low number of skin cancer cases and lack of treatment-specific data should be acknowledged. Future research in larger cohorts may further clarify these findings, particularly in subpopulations with overlapping risk factors.

In conclusion, our results indicate that MGUS does not confer an increased risk of skin cancer or affect skin cancer-specific survival. This information provides reassurance for clinicians managing individuals with MGUS and emphasizes the importance of evidence-based guidelines for dermatological care in this population.
