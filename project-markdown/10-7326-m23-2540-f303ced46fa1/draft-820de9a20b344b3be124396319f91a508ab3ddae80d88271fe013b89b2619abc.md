### Abstract

Background: Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) are asymptomatic precursor conditions to multiple myeloma and related disorders. Smoldering multiple myeloma is distinguished from MGUS by 10% or greater bone marrow plasma cells (BMPC) on sampling, has a higher risk for progression, and requires specialist management.

Objective: To develop a multivariable prediction model that predicts the probability that a person with presumed MGUS has 10% or greater BMPC (SMM or worse by bone marrow criteria) to inform the decision to obtain a bone marrow sample and compare its performance to the Mayo Clinic risk stratification model.

Design: iStopMM (Iceland Screens, Treats or Prevents Multiple Myeloma), a prospective population-based screening study of MGUS. (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03327597)

Setting: Icelandic population of adults aged 40 years or older.

Patients: 1043 persons with IgG, IgA, light-chain, and biclonal MGUS detected by screening and an interpretable bone marrow sample.

Measurements: Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance isotype; monoclonal protein concentration; free light-chain ratio; and total IgG, IgM, and IgA concentrations were used as predictors. Bone marrow plasma cells were categorized as 0% to 4%, 5% to 9%, 10% to 14%, or 15% or greater.

Results: The c-statistic for SMM or worse was 0.85 (95% CI, 0.82 to 0.88), and calibration was excellent (intercept, −0.07; slope, 0.95). At a threshold of 10% predicted risk for SMM or worse, sensitivity was 86%, specificity was 67%, positive predictive value was 32%, and negative predictive value was 96%. Compared with the Mayo Clinic model, the net benefit for the decision to refer for sampling was between 0.13 and 0.30 higher over a range of plausible low-risk thresholds.

Limitation: The prediction model will require external validation.

Conclusion: This accurate prediction model for SMM or worse was developed in a population-based cohort of persons with presumed MGUS and may be used to defer bone marrow sampling and referral to hematology.

Primary Funding Source: International Myeloma Foundation and the European Research Council.

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is a common asymptomatic precursor condition to multiple myeloma (MM) and other lymphoproliferative disorders (1). Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance is defined by the presence of monoclonal (M) protein at a concentration lower than 30 g/L or an abnormal free light-chain (FLC) ratio with increased levels of the involved light-chain, fewer than 10% bone marrow plasma cells (BMPC), and the absence of a myeloma-defining event (2, 3). Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance is present in 4.2% of adults aged 50 years or older (4, 5). The overall rate of progression to MM or other related disorders is 0.5% to 1% per year, and most persons with MGUS never progress (6).

Several laboratory parameters can predict progression of MGUS, including the involved immunoglobulin isotype, M protein concentration, and FLC ratio (7–9). These were combined in the Mayo Clinic risk stratification model, which defined risk groups on the basis of the number of abnormal parameters (8, 9). In the derivation cohort, the average absolute risk for progression at 20 years was 5%, 21%, 37%, and 58% in groups with 0, 1, 2, or all 3 parameters abnormal, respectively (9). However, even in the high-risk group, most (73%) persons had not progressed at 20 years after accounting for the competing risk for death (9). Clinical guidelines recommend lifelong monitoring of persons with MGUS for all risk groups (10). Previous studies have shown that those who were being monitored at the time of progression had fewer complications and longer overall survival after diagnosis of MM (11, 12). The optimal intensity of monitoring is unknown and is currently being investigated in the iStopMM (Iceland Screens, Treats or Prevents Multiple Myeloma) study (13).

Smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) is an asymptomatic intermediate state between MGUS and MM that is defined by the presence of BMPC between 10% and 59% or a M protein concentration of 30 g/L or greater, in the absence of a myeloma-defining event (2). Smoldering multiple myeloma is less common than MGUS, with a prevalence of 0.5% in persons aged 40 years and older (14). However, the risk for progression to MM or related disorders is considerably higher at 10% per year for the first 5 years after diagnosis (15, 16). Due to the much higher risk for progression, guidelines recommend intense monitoring and regular imaging studies of persons with SMM (10). Randomized clinical trials involving persons with high-risk SMM show that early intervention is associated with longer time to progression and improved overall survival in 1 study (17, 18).

A bone marrow sample is required to distinguish between persons with presumed MGUS and SMM. However, bone marrow sampling is a resource intensive procedure that can be painful and is generally only done at specialized centers (19). Guidelines suggest that most persons with MGUS should undergo bone marrow sampling but that the procedure may be deferred in those classified as low-risk MGUS according to the Mayo Clinic model (10). ### METHODS

Ethical Considerations

Approvals from the National Bioethics Committee (NBC 16-022-04-26-2016) and the Data Protection Authority in Iceland as well as written consent from all participants were obtained.

Data Sources and Study Population

The iStopMM study invited all Icelanders born in 1975 or earlier and alive on 9 September 2016 to participate in a screening study of MGUS. In total, 75 422 participants provided a screening blood sample that was shipped to The Binding Site in Birmingham, United Kingdom, for serum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation electrophoresis, and serum FLC analysis (13). Participants with abnormal screening test results consistent with MGUS entered a randomized controlled trial. Group 1 continued to receive care provided by the Icelandic Health Service as though they had never been screened. Groups 2 and 3 were evaluated at the study clinic for initial assessment and follow-up, including bone marrow sampling (10). Trephine biopsies stained with hematoxylin and eosin and immunohistochemically stained for CD138 were evaluated by 2 hematopathologists. Bone marrow smears were stained with Giemsa stain and jointly evaluated by 2 hematologists. The sample with the higher percentage of BMPC infiltration was used. A more detailed description of the study design has been published elsewhere (13).

The current study cohort included all persons with presumed MGUS randomly assigned to groups 2 and 3 of the iStopMM study. Excluded were persons with IgM MGUS (does not progress to MM), those who were randomly assigned to group 2 and categorized as low-risk MGUS according to the Mayo Clinic risk stratification model (protocol did not include bone marrow sampling), those who never presented to the study clinic after randomization, those who never had bone marrow sampling, those who had bone marrow sampling more than 365 days from the screening blood sample, and persons whose trephine biopsy and bone marrow aspirate results were both uninterpretable with regards to the percentage of BMPC.

as 0% to 4%, 5% to 9%, 10% to 14%, or 15% or higher. In our cohort of presumed MGUS, the outcome of 10% or greater BMPC identifies persons with SMM or worse and is called “SMM or worse” for the remainder of this article.

Predictor variables were obtained from the screening blood sample. Monoclonal protein concentration and FLC ratio were modeled as nonlinear variables using four-knot restricted cubic splines with knots placed at the 0.05, 0.35, 0.65, and 0.95 percentiles. Total IgG, IgA, and IgM concentrations were modeled as linear variables. Isotype was modeled as a categorical variable, with the following categories: IgG MGUS, IgA MGUS, light-chain MGUS, and biclonal MGUS.

A formal minimum sample size calculation was done (20). Assuming that 15% of the cohort had SMM or worse by bone marrow criteria and assuming 12 candidate predictor variables, the estimated minimum required sample size was 1008 persons (Supplement Table 1, available at Annals.org). No missing data were present. The proportional odds assumptions were explored graphically, and penalized maximum likelihood estimation was considered over a range of penalty factors (0, 0.5, and 1) and values chosen to maximize the corrected Akaike information criterion. Uniform shrinkage estimated via bootstrapping was applied to the linear predictor of the final model (21).

Evaluation of the Prediction Model for SMM or Worse by Bone Marrow Criteria

The Mayo Clinic risk stratification model was fit as a logistic model with 10% or greater BMPC as an outcome and used for comparison to the prediction model (9, 10). The Mayo Clinic model classifies persons with MGUS into risk groups on the basis of 3 cutoffs: IgG MGUS, M protein concentration below 15 g/L, and FLC ratio between 0.26 and 1.65. Persons are classified as low-risk, low-intermediate risk, high-intermediate risk, and high-risk MGUS when 0, 1, 2, or all 3 conditions are unmet, respectively (8, 9).

Internal validation of both models was done by presenting optimism-corrected indices of fit, discrimination, and calibration. Optimism is estimated by repeatedly refitting a model to bootstrap resamples with replacement from the original cohort and subtracting their performance in the original cohort from their performance in their respective bootstrap samples. Optimism correction involves subtracting the optimism from performance indices of the original model, after which they better approximate the expected performance of the model when applied to a new sample. The overall model fit was quantified with Nagelkerke R2 (a measure of goodness of fit ranging from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect predictions) and Brier scores (a quadratic scoring rule, for which 0 represents perfect accuracy). Discrimination was quantified using the c-statistic (the probability that for any pair of individuals, of whom 1 had SMM or worse and the other did not, a higher risk is predicted for the individual with SMM or worse). Calibration, the agreement between observed and predicted risks, was assessed by visual examination of calibration plots and several calibration indices (overall calibration intercept and slope and Emax). Optimism-corrected CIs were constructed by refitting the models to 2000 bootstrap resamples with replacement (21, 22). The lower and upper limits of the CI were defined as the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of the resulting distributions. Calibration was further evaluated in subgroups of age, sex, and MGUS isotype.

### RESULTS

### Study Population

In total, 2104 persons who were diagnosed with IgG, IgA, light-chain, or biclonal MGUS on screening and randomly assigned to groups 2 and 3 of the iStopMM trial were eligible for inclusion. Of those, 1061 were excluded, and the remaining 1043 persons made up the study cohort (Figure 1). Median age was 68 years (IQR, 60 to 75 years), and 578 (55.4%) were men. A total of 158 (15.1%) persons had 10% or greater BMPC on sampling, of whom 156 (98.7%) had SMM and 2 (1.3%) had MM. The characteristics of the study population are presented in the Table.

Performance of the Prediction Model

The prediction model included MGUS isotype; M protein; FLC ratio; and total concentrations of IgG, IgA, and IgM as predictors. Visual examination of diagnostic figures suggested that the proportional odds assumptions were met (Supplement Figures 1 and 2, available at Annals.org). The median predicted risk for SMM or worse by bone marrow criteria in our cohort was 7.7% (IQR, 4.3% to 17.4%). Compared with IgG MGUS, the risk for SMM or worse was only slightly higher for biclonal MGUS (odds ratio \[OR\], 1.64 \[95% CI, 1.11 to 2.44\]), IgA MGUS (OR, 1.48 \[CI, 0.98 to 2.23\]), and light-chain MGUS (OR, 1.04 \[CI, 0.60 to 1.80\]). By far the 2 strongest predictors of SMM or worse were M protein concentration (OR between 3.9 g/L \[75th percentile\] and 2.1 g/L \[median\], 2.07 \[CI, 1.63 to 2.63 g/L\]) and FLC ratio (OR between 1.9 \[75th percentile\] and 1.2 \[median\], 1.29 \[CI, 1.13 to 1.47\]) (Supplement Figure 3, available at Annals.org).

The c-statistic of the prediction model for SMM or worse was 0.85 (CI, 0.82 to 0.89) (Supplement Table 2, available at Annals.org). Visual examination of the calibration plots showed excellent calibration with no systemic tendencies to under- or overestimate risk (Figure 2). ### DISCUSSION

We have developed a prediction model that accurately predicts the presence of 10% or more BMPC and therefore a diagnosis of SMM or MM by bone marrow criteria in persons presumed to have MGUS. Although the outcome of 10% or greater BMPC defines both SMM and MM by bone marrow criteria, the predicted probabilities obtained from our model may be assumed to apply to a diagnosis of SMM, as only 2 persons were diagnosed with MM in our cohort. We show that our model exhibits excellent discrimination with a c-statistic of 0.85 for SMM or worse and displays superb calibration over the entire range of predicted risk (Figure 2). For any plausible low-risk threshold, our prediction model identifies a higher proportion of persons with MGUS as being low-risk for SMM or worse with a higher negative predictive value than does the Mayo Clinic risk stratification model, and consequently the net benefit of our model is always higher by a margin of 0.13 to 0.30 (Figures 3 and 4). In this context, a net benefit of 0.30 is consistent with a strategy that reduces the number of unnecessary bone marrow sampling procedures by 300 for every 1000 persons with presumed MGUS, without missing a single additional case of SMM or worse. This is in addition to the net benefit already provided by the Mayo Clinic model compared with a strategy of obtaining a bone marrow sample in all persons.

Our model leverages laboratory parameters commonly available at the time of diagnosis of presumed MGUS and could therefore be used in the clinic to facilitate shared decision making regarding referral for bone marrow sampling. Persons with predicted risks below the relevant low-risk threshold could safely defer bone marrow sampling and be managed by primary care physicians as presumed MGUS. Importantly, this model will need to be externally validated before clinical use. The model does not supersede clinical judgment and should only be used if there is no clinical suspicion of MM or related lymphoproliferative disorders, including light-chain amyloidosis.

Bone marrow sampling, although a relatively safe procedure, requires an additional visit to a specially trained health care provider after which a hematopathologist must examine the sample. The procedure itself can be painful and is associated with rare complications (19). Because MGUS is present in 4.2% of adults aged 50 years and older (4, 5), a strategy that includes routine bone marrow sampling to screen for SMM or worse could expose a substantial proportion of the adult population to a potentially unnecessary and painful procedure. Efforts should be made to identify persons who are unlikely to benefit from the procedure to minimize harm and costs. Classically, such decisions have been supported by the creation of risk groups, of which the Mayo Clinic risk stratification model is an example (8, 9). The model was originally developed to stratify persons with MGUS into risk groups on the basis of their absolute risk for progression to MM or related disorders at 20 years. Since then, advances in imaging and changes in the definition of MM have likely rendered these risk estimates inaccurate (2). In current clinical practice, the model has been repurposed on the basis of expert opinion for use in the decision to obtain a bone marrow sample with the recommendation that sampling may be deferred in persons classified as having low-risk MGUS. This was partially justified on the grounds that only 4.8% of those classified as low-risk MGUS in the derivation cohort turned out to have 10% or greater BMPC (10). In our screened, prospective, population-based cohort, the Mayo Clinic model had a c-statistic of 0.67, indicating poor discrimination, yet outperformed a strategy of obtaining a bone marrow sample in all persons, with net benefit ranging from 0.0 to 0.26 at low-risk thresholds of 5% to 20%. At a risk threshold of 10%, using the Mayo Clinic model to defer bone marrow sampling compared with sampling all persons was consistent with a strategy that reduced the number of unnecessary bone marrow sampling procedures by 140 for every 1000 persons with presumed MGUS, without missing a single additional case of SMM or worse (Figure 4). Even so, using a risk group approach for individual-level decision making has several drawbacks. The Mayo Clinic model groups together persons with large variations in their predicted risk for SMM. Participants who were classified as low-risk MGUS and predicted to have 6.2% risk for SMM or worse according to the Mayo Clinic model had individual risk predictions ranging from 1.5% and 69% using our prediction model.

Instead of basing recommendations on risk groups, we propose a new paradigm with recommendations based on each individual’s predicted risks. This offers several advantages to the risk group approach: using predicted risks rather than fixed risk groups facilitates shared decision making between the clinician and the patient, allowing for personalized care of MGUS by applying different risk thresholds depending on each unique case; using predicted risk is invariant to how the risk was derived and does not force the use of arbitrary cutoffs; individual clinics or health services could prioritize their waiting lists on the basis of the rank of the predicted risks, always ensuring that those with the highest probability of SMM have the shortest waiting time; international societies could base their guidance on bone marrow sampling or referral to hematology clinic on low-risk thresholds rather than specific combinations of risk factors; the model is easy to use, does not require any postprocessing of laboratory results, and has been made available as a clinical calculator at www.istopmm.com/riskmodel; and if new biomarkers are later discovered, they can be tested and added to the prediction model and all of the same intuitions and recommendations regarding thresholds would still apply.

Our model has possible weaknesses that must be addressed. Although sample size was adequate and bootstrap resampling with optimism correction was used to combat overfitting, our model will need to be externally validated in other screened and clinical cohorts to ensure generalizability. This is especially important as our model was developed in the predominantly White and genetically homogeneous population of Iceland. Furthermore, many clinicians and persons with MGUS may want to base their decision on whether to obtain a bone marrow sample on the probability of 20% or greater BMPC, as this is a component of the International Myeloma Working Group 2/20/20 risk stratification model for SMM (26). The proportional odds logistic model framework allows the same model to predict the probability of several monotonically related outcomes. Indeed, our model currently predicts the probability of 5% or greater, 10% or greater, and 15% or greater BMPC, although the current work focuses exclusively on the 10% or greater BMPC outcome. In our cohort, only 32 (3.1%) persons had 20% or greater BMPC, and incorporating this outcome introduced instability into the bootstrap resampling procedure and was therefore not included. It may be argued that the risk group approach of the 2/20/20 model for SMM will have all of the same problems as the Mayo Clinic model for MGUS.

However, as with the 10% or greater BMPC threshold, we acknowledge that 20% or greater BMPC has acquired important meaning in the myeloma community and would be a worthy addition to the prediction model. This outcome could easily be incorporated if a larger data set were available for model updating. Finally, and most importantly, our study does not provide evidence that identifying persons with SMM and monitoring them more intensely would result in superior outcomes. In fact, no prospective study has shown that monitoring of MGUS or SMM improves outcomes of MM after progression. This is a critical research question in the field of monoclonal gammopathies and is the main subject of the iStopMM trial.

Our study provides an evidence-based approach to selecting persons with presumed MGUS in whom bone marrow sampling may be deferred and has the potential to both decrease the number of persons unnecessarily exposed to this safe but invasive procedure by facilitating shared decision making, allowing for personalized care of MGUS by applying different risk thresholds depending on each unique case. However, our model requires validation in other populations.
