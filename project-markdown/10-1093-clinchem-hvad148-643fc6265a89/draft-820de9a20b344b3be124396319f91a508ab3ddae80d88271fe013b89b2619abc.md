Screening in Multiple Myeloma and Its Precursors: Are We There Yet?

Sæmundur Rögnvaldsson,a,b,\* Sigrún Thorsteinsdóttir,a,c and Sigurður Yngvi Kristinssona,b

BACKGROUND: Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy that develops over years from the asymptomatic precursors, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, and smoldering multiple myeloma. Recent evidence shows that by initiating treatment at an asymptomatic stage, outcomes in MM can be significantly improved. However, a vast majority of MM patients are diagnosed after the development of symptomatic end-organ damage and cannot reap the benefits of early treatment. The precursors of MM are easily detected by serum protein electrophoresis and free light chain assay of the serum, raising the question of whether population-based screening could detect MM at an asymptomatic stage and significantly expand the availability of early treatment in MM. Screening is a hallmark of care in many malignancies, and there are accepted criteria for when screening is appropriate.

CONTENT: Here we review the available relevant evidence for the introduction of screening and discuss whether screening for MM and its precursors fulfills these criteria. We also highlight gaps in our current knowledge, most notably a lack of data on the benefits and harms of screening and the lack of a defined target population. There are ongoing studies that may fill these critical gaps in the literature, but their results are still pending.

SUMMARY: Screening could lead to a paradigm shift in the care of patients with MM, but critical scientific questions need to be answered before screening of healthy individuals can be recommended. In short, we should not screen for MM and its precursors—yet.

MM is characterized by monoclonal immunoglobulins (M proteins) and/or free light chains (FLC) in the serum alongside its clinical hallmarks of hypercalcemia, renal disease, anemia, bone disease, or myeloma-defining events (Fig. 1) (2). Historically, prognosis in MM has been poor, but with the advent of novel therapeutics around the turn of the 21st century, survival has improved dramatically (3). Even so, MM remains a major cause of mortality and morbidity leading to an estimated 2.1 million disability-adjusted life-years being lost in 2016 (4).

All cases of MM are preceded by monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) (6, 7). MGUS is an asymptomatic condition defined by the presence of M proteins or an abnormal FLC ratio in the absence of active MM or related disorders (8). Because MGUS is asymptomatic, a majority of cases go undetected until they progress to symptomatic MM or related disease. Screening studies have shown that MGUS is common in the general population with a prevalence of 4.2% in individuals of mostly Scandinavian descent residing in Olmsted County in Minnesota in the US over the age of 50 and is more prevalent in men than women (5.1 vs 3.5%) (9, 10). The prevalence of MGUS is even higher in those of African descent (11, 12) and increases significantly with age (9, 10). It has, however, been reported to be rarer in Asian populations in China (13) and India (14). These differences appear to be driven by genetic predisposition with multiple studies showing that MM and MGUS is more than 2 times as common in first-degree relatives of MM patients and individuals with MGUS (11, 15, 16), and common risk alleles have been identified at 25 independent loci (17–21). Several environmental risk factors for MGUS been described, including exposure to pesticides (22) and radiation (23), obesity, and chronic antigen stimulation including infections and inflammatory conditions (24).

Early Intervention in MM

Current consensus guidelines recommend indefinite follow-up of individuals with MGUS and SMM with the aim of detecting progression to MM (8). There are currently no randomized studies supporting this approach. However, there are at least 3 large observational studies of MM patients from Sweden and the US, showing that those with previously known MGUS had less end-organ disease at MM diagnosis and had a 13% to 15% increase in overall survival (Fig. 2) (37–39). These improvements are likely due to the earlier detection and intervention in MM indicating that follow-up of MGUS can improve outcomes in MM. Interestingly, this evidence is contradicted by a smaller study from Israel showing that delayed initiation of treatment in symptomatic MM did not affect survival, suggesting that intervention before the advent of symptomatic disease is not needed to improve outcomes (40).

The idea of initiating treatment even earlier, at an asymptomatic stage, has been enticing for some time with the hope of preventing MM related end-organ disease and even curing the disease. Early trials of alkylating agents, corticosteroids, and bisphosphonates were discouraging, showing no difference in progression-free or overall survival but significant toxicities (41–44).

With the advent of novel therapeutics in the new millennium, there are now 3 trials that show an improvement in outcomes by initiating treatment at the stage of SMM. The first trial was published in 2013 by Witzig et al. and showed that treatment with thalidomide and zoledronic acid increased progression-free as compared to zoledronic acid alone with no difference in overall survival (45). The second and most notable trial was published the same year by Mateos et al. from Spain showing that treating patients with high-risk SMM for 2 years with lenalidomide and dexamethasone improved both progression-free and overall survival as compared to observation (46). An updated analysis was recently published showing that this improvement persisted even after 12 years of follow-up with treatment improving overall survival by 43% (hazards ratio = 0.57; Fig. 3) (47). These findings are further supported by the third trial published in 2020 by Lonial et al. (the ECOG trial), which found that indefinite lenalidomide monotherapy led to improved progression-free and lower rates of end-organ damage but no improvement in overall survival. Treatment of SMM has met some criticism since individuals with SMM are asymptomatic and many of those treated would never have developed MM, in which case treatment is not only not useful but potentially harmful (51). Furthermore, definitions of high-risk SMM varies and are often contradictory, and there is no general consensus on which definitions should guide the application of therapy (32). Critics also note that an improvement in overall survival, arguably the single most important endpoint, has hitherto only been observed in a single trial that was conducted before implementation of the new myeloma-defining events (52, 53). Despite this, out of the 32 currently registered clinical trials of treatment in SMM, there is only one unpublished ongoing phase 3 trial comparing treatment to observation (NCT03301220) while 2 more recent ongoing phase 3 trials include therapy with lenalidomide and dexamethasone in the control arm (NCT03937635 and NCT04270409). The application of early treatment in MM is severely limited by the asymptomatic nature of MGUS and SMM. Only 4% to 7% of those who develop MM have previously known precursor disease (37–39), and the median time from the symptomatic onset of MM to diagnosis has been found to be 3 to 6 months (56, 57). This means that 93% to 96% of MM patients cannot receive early treatment and wait for the onset of end-organ damage that can be debilitating, be irreversible, and limit the application of effective therapies. Because these asymptomatic precursors of MM can be detected by a simple blood sample, this raises the question of whether it is feasible to use population-wide screening to detect early-MM, SMM, and MGUS and provide early therapy to those who would benefit from it.

Is Screening Appropriate in MM?

The original and emerging Wilson and Jungner criteria criteria can be used as a framework to review the case for

MM screening. Applying the criteria to MM screening, it can be observed that many criteria are indeed fulfilled. However, there remain some critical questions that have not been answered.

MM is an important health problem (original criteria 1) leading to significant morbidity, mortality (4), and costs (83). When treatment is warranted, there are available and accepted options (original criteria 2). Facilities for diagnosis and treatment are widely available (original criteria 3), as they are part of standard hematological practice. The precursor condition is easily identifiable (original criteria 4), and the natural history of MGUS progression to MM is well understood and extensively studied (original criteria 7) (25).

Screening could be a means to significantly expand the access of MM patients to early treatment, something that has only been available for a small minority. Screening, therefore, does respond to a recognized need (emerging criteria 1), and the objective, i.e., finding individuals with MM precursors and finding those

The screening test is a simple blood draw to collect a serum sample for serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and FLC assay with follow-up immunofixation electrophoresis if abnormal. These tests reliably identify clinically meaningful precursor disease, and further diagnostic workup, including bone marrow sampling, may then identify those who have actionable disease (6, 7). However, many of the MGUS cases detected during screening will not progress, and some may not even have any underlying premalignant processes, particularly in the case with abnormal FLC assay results where impaired renal function can lead to false-positive results. Renal reference ranges for FLC measurements have recently been published, significantly improving the accuracy of these measurements in clinical use (84). Importantly, treatment is not necessarily indicated for those who have a positive screening test and can be deferred with non- or low-invasive testing. The screening test is therefore both a suitable screening test (original criteria 5) and, since it is a simple blood draw, acceptable to the population (original criteria 6) and easily repeatable (original criteria 10).

Some of the criteria (emerging criteria 5–9) pertain to the organization and implementation of screening programs. The issues raised, including maintaining equity, quality assurance, confidentiality, informed consent, and more, are critical features of any screening program that is effective and ethical. However, they are practical and organizational ones and do not require further scientific debate.

There remain criteria that are currently not fulfilled for MM screening. First, there is no published evidence on the effectiveness of MM screening or its potential harms (emerging criteria 2). Therefore, it is impossible to evaluate whether the overall benefits of screening outweigh the harm (emerging criteria 10). Second, a target population for screening has not been defined (emerging criteria 3). Third, the costs of screening, follow-up, and treatment for MM screening have not been estimated formally, and, because the benefits and harms of screening are not known, whether the benefits of MM screening outweigh the costs is not known (original criteria 9). Fulfilling these missing criteria is imperative in order to initiate any type of screening. Therefore, with the exception of rigorous scientific studies, we should not initiate or recommend screening for MM and its precursors at this time.

The Missing Pieces

MGUS and SMM affect a nontrivial portion of the total population and are asymptomatic disorders. Importantly, a majority of those affected will never

develop overt disease precluding any potential benefits and leaving only potential harms from screening. Screening leads to overdiagnosis, unneeded testing, and overtreatment; could potentially lead to unwarranted anxiety, depressed quality of life, and even psychiatric illness; and incurs significant costs for healthcare systems and individuals. Before initiating MM screening on a systematic level, the extent of these potential harms of screening needs to be studied and weighed against the potential benefits of early diagnosis and intervention in MM. The only way to do this is by performing randomized studies.

Determining whether the benefits of screening outweigh the harms is in no way trivial or obvious. The most meaningful endpoint of screening benefit is overall survival. It can even be argued that overall survival is the only meaningful endpoint of screening. However, who should have an overall survival benefit for screening to be worthwhile? Is it enough to observe that those who develop SMM or MM have an improved overall survival from having been screened, or should all those who have a positive screening or even all those who undergo screening have an improved overall survival from screening? In practice it can be difficult to evaluate the role of screening in SMM and MM survival due to lead-time bias, and enormous populations would be needed to power a study with overall survival of those who are screened as an endpoint. Because the potential harms of the screening test itself are negligible, it is rational to focus on the overall survival of those who have a positive screening test (i.e., have MGUS). However, due to the low progression rate of MGUS and the constantly improving survival rate in MM (3), it is possible that differences in overall survival will not be observed in less than 1 or 2 decades. On the other hand, screening could lead to the prevention of MM-related end-organ disease and the associated symptom burden related to end-organ damage and decrease the need for prolonged or aggressive therapy, which could lead to an improved quality of life. If that is the case, these endpoints could also be viewed as meaningful benefits of screening. Importantly, progression-free is not necessarily a good marker of screening benefit because it includes biochemical progression, which is not meaningful to quality of life. Instead, general and specific quality of life measures and clinical endpoints like fractures or infections should be used to evaluate these less determined benefits. These benefits need to be viewed in context of the potential harms of screening, and these “softer” benefits of screening are only acceptable if the harms are minimal.

As discussed earlier, the prevalence of MGUS differs significantly across age groups, ethnicities, families, and various environmental risk factors, and these risk factors may need to be taken into account when defining a target population for screening. The median age at MM diagnosis is around 71 years, and only around 1% of cases present before the age of 40 (1). In order to capture a significant portion of MM cases early, screening therefore needs to start well before the age of 70, but screening before the age of 40 is likely to have a low yield. At the other end of the age spectrum, the prevalence of MGUS increases significantly, but, due to shorter life expectancy, individuals are less likely to progress from MGUS to overt MM in their lifetime. Furthermore, older or frail individuals are less likely to want or tolerate early treatment, limiting the benefits of screening in older subgroups. Sex, ethnicity, and genetic predisposition will also be important in defining a target population for screening. Males have a higher prevalence of MGUS as compared to females (5.1% vs 3.5%) (9, 10). Black individuals are around 2 times as likely to have MGUS as those who are White (12), and Black MM patients are younger than White MM patients, with a median age at diagnosis of 66 years (85). Similarly, first-degree relatives of MM patients and individuals with MGUS have more than double the risk of MGUS and MM (11, 15, 16). Conversely, Asian populations have far lower rates of MM and MGUS (13, 14). A target population or differential age-defined target populations for MM screening should therefore be identified using sex, ethnicity, and family history.

It is important to frame the intervention of MM screening not as the screening test itself, which is simple and noninvasive, but rather as the follow-up for those with a positive screening test. It is during follow-up that individuals undergo invasive testing and/or treatment, which can lead to benefits but also harms. Populations with a high prevalence of MGUS are therefore not necessarily those who benefit the most from screening. The obvious example is the elderly where the prevalence of MGUS is high but, due to frailty and a shortened lifespan, early treatment would not be appropriate, and the benefits of screening would be minimal despite the high prevalence of MGUS. Similarly, there is some recent data indicating that Black individuals with SMM may have a lower risk of progression to MM (86). Thus, it could be argued that screening would lead to fewer benefits in Black individuals since they would be more likely to be harmed by overtreatment. However, more studies are required on the ethnic and genetic factors affecting progression risk in MGUS and SMM. Most importantly, it will be important to consider individual preferences regarding whether they

These preferences will differ between individuals, cultures, and healthcare systems, and it is unlikely that one universal screening program will be the right one everywhere.

iStopMM and Other Ongoing Studies

We are currently undertaking the first large-scale population-based screening study in Iceland, the Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents Multiple Myeloma Study (iStopMM, ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03327597). It is currently the only randomized study assessing the benefits and harms of screening for MM and its precursors. A total of 75 422 Icelanders aged 40 years and over have now been screened by SPEP and FLC assay and immunofixation electrophoresis in those with a suspected M protein on SPEP or an abnormal FLC ratio. Those who have MGUS have been randomized to 3 arms. Two-thirds of participants (arms 2 and 3) are contacted and called in for diagnostics and follow-up while a crucial third of participants are not contacted and function as a control arm and continue with their lives as though they were never screened (Fig. 4). Participants who develop intermediate- or high-risk SMM or active MM are offered early treatment in a phase 2 single-arm trial (87). By comparing arm 1 to arms 2 and 3, it will be possible to objectively assess the benefits and harms of screening. The primary outcome of the study is overall survival, but endpoints like MM-related end-organ damage, need for MM treatment, MM specific survival, and multiple quality of life metrics will also be assessed. Despite these encouraging results, more time is needed to evaluate whether this increased detection of disease will impact overall survival or quality of life. Furthermore, the Icelandic population is genetically homogenous and mostly White, and studies of other populations are also needed. In the US, another screening study, the Predicting Progression of Developing Myeloma in a High-risk Screened Population (PROMISE, ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03689595) study, is ongoing and still recruiting. The PROMISE study is a nonrandomized cohort study including Black individuals and individuals with a family history of hematological cancers. Participants are screened for MM by SPEP, immunofixation electrophoresis, and FLC assay as well as more sensitive mass spectrometry-based diagnostics. Participants are followed over time with repeated blood samples with the primary endpoint being time to progression (89). Due to the nonrandomized nature of the PROMISE study, the results of the study will not be useful in determining the benefits or harms of screening, but, due to the different study populations, the study will be integral in defining a target population for MM screening.

Concluding Remarks

There is mounting evidence that identifying MM and its precursors and intervening at an asymptomatic stage can improve outcomes in MM, and even though there remains some ambiguity on what subgroup of patients truly benefit from early treatment, treating some with asymptomatic disease is becoming the standard-of-care. The asymptomatic nature of the disease prior to end-organ damage makes it impossible to apply such early treatments on a large scale, and its benefits can therefore

136 Clinical Chemistry 70:1 (2024)

only be realized in a small subset of MM patients where asymptomatic disease has been incidentally diagnosed. Screening for MM and its precursors could radically expand the availability of early treatment in MM and has the potential to evoke a major paradigm shift in MM care from late diagnosis at the point of end-organ damage to early detection, early intervention, and potentially prevention of MM-related end-organ disease. When applying the Wilson and Jungner criteria, a widely accepted criteria for when disease screening is appropriate, it can be observed that MM and its precursors have, in many ways, the attributes of diseases where screening is appropriate. However, critical questions remain unanswered. Most notably, there are currently no published studies evaluating the benefits and harms of screening, including its financial costs. Furthermore, a target population for screening has not and cannot be defined based on the currently available data. There are some clinical situations where MM screening is appropriate, for example in close families with multiple cases of MM, but only after counseling about the uncertainty of benefit and the potential harms, including the “financial toxicities” of detecting a precursor condition. However, the current general recommendation should be to refrain from MM screening in healthy individuals. Randomized trials are needed if this recommendation is to change in the future. The iStopMM study is the only ongoing randomized study evaluating the benefits and harms of MM screening. Early results show that screening is feasible and increases the detection of treatable disease, but the long-term benefits or the potential harms of screening have not yet been established. The study will mature in the coming years, and if MM screening leads to improved overall survival or prevents symptomatic disease and improves quality of life without significant harms, screening should become the standard of care. But until then, we should not screen for MM and its precursors.
