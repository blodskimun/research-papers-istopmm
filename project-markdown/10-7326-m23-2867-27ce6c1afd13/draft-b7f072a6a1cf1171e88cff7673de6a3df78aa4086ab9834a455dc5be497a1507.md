Background: Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined signiﬁcance (MGUS) is a precursor of multiple myeloma (MM) and related conditions. In previous registry-based, retrospective studies, autoimmune diseases have been associated with MGUS. However, these studies were not based on a screened population and are therefore prone to ascertainment bias.

Objective: To examine whether MGUS is associated with autoimmune diseases.

Design: A cross-sectional study within iStopMM (Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents MM), a prospective, population-based screening study of MGUS.

Setting: Icelandic population of adults aged 40 years or older.

Patients: 75 422 persons screened for MGUS.

Measurements: Poisson regression for prevalence ratios (PRs) of MGUS among persons with or without an autoimmune disease, adjusted for age and sex.

Results: A total of 10 818 participants had an autoimmune disorder, of whom 599 had MGUS (61 with a prior clinical diagnosis and 538 diagnosed at study

screening or evaluation). A diagnosis of an autoimmune disease was not associated with MGUS (PR, 1.05 \[95% CI, 0.97 to 1.15\]). However, autoimmune disease diagnoses were associated with a prior clinical diagnosis of MGUS (PR, 2.11 \[CI, 1.64 to 2.70\]).

Limitation: Registry data were used to gather information on autoimmune diseases, and the homogeneity of the Icelandic population may limit the generalizability of these results.

Conclusion: The study did not ﬁnd an association between autoimmune disease and MGUS in a systematically screened population. Previous studies not done in systematically screened populations have likely been subject to ascertainment bias. The ﬁndings indicate that recommendations to routinely screen patients with autoimmune disease for MGUS may not be warranted.

onoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and light-chain MGUS (LC-MGUS) are asymptomatic, premalignant plasma cell disorders that precede multiple myeloma (MM) and other related disorders (1–3). Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined signiﬁcance affects 3% to 4% of persons older than 50 years, whereas LC-MGUS has a prevalence of 1% (4). Each year, approximately 1% of patients with MGUS and 0.3% of patients with LC-MGUS progress to MM or other malignant conditions (4, 5). The cause of MGUS is largely unknown. However, a few risk factors have been established, such as male sex, age (6), Black race (7), pesticide exposure (8), and a family history of MGUS (9). In addition, several retrospective studies have found an increased risk for MGUS among patients with autoimmune diseases and immune-related conditions (10–12). One hypothesis is that chronic inﬂammation triggers an immune response that ultimately leads

to MGUS or even cancer (13). It has alternatively been proposed that treatment of autoimmune diseases increases the risk for a plasma cell disorder, or that the presence of a common genetic or environmental factor predisposes persons to developing both autoimmune disease and plasma cell dyscrasia (10, 11, 14, 15). Furthermore, hematologic cancer has been linked to autoimmune disease, especially lymphoproliferative disorders, with previous studies showing an association with both non-Hodgkin and Hodgkin lymphoma (16). A particularly strong association has been described between monoclonal gammopathies and primary Sjögren syndrome, with the presence of MGUS being a marker of disease activity associated with a poor prog-nosis (20). However, most studies were based on retrospective cohorts identiﬁed through medical records (10–12, 14) where patients are diagnosed through clinical work-up for other diseases, introducing ascertainment bias. Even though these studies included up to 5400 MGUS cases (12), none were based on systematic screening. Because patients seeking medical care are more likely to get tested for various conditions, including MGUS, these studies have a built-in ascertainment bias. A recent study using data from the iStopMM (Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents MM) study has conﬁrmed this, showing that persons diagnosed with MGUS outside screening were more likely to have various comorbid conditions, including rheumatologic disease, neurologic disease, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, heart failure, and endocrine disorders (21). This ascertainment bias has likely affected most previous studies, possibly leading to an overesti-mation of the association between MGUS and autoimmune diseases. This notion is supported by the results of a moderately sized screening study for MGUS that included 17398 participants with 605 MGUS cases where no association was seen between MGUS and autoimmune diseases (22). However, a larger study is needed. Therefore, we used data from the iStopMM study, a population-based study in which more than 75000

persons from the general population have been screened for both MGUS and LC-MGUS, to perform a cross-sectional study of the association between autoimmune diseases and MGUS.

METHODS Ethical Considerations The National Bioethics Committee (VSN 16-022; 26 April 2016) and the Data Protection Authority in Iceland have approved the iStopMM study and related data collection that is part of the study. All participants gave their informed, written consent.

Study Design, iStopMM and Recruitment of Participants, and Data Sources This was a cross-sectional study using data from iStopMM, a nationwide randomized clinical trial that aimed to investigate the beneﬁts and harms of screening for MGUS. All 148 704 registered residents of Iceland as of September 2016 who were born in 1975 or earlier were invited to enroll in the study. Potential participants were invited through a direct mailing campaign, which included a brochure about the study. In addition, a tradi-tional and social media advertising campaign was used to reach a broader audience and direct potential participants to the study website. Study Sample and Eligibility Criteria The current study included all 75422 participants who were screened for MGUS in the iStopMM study. All persons who had a prior diagnosis of MM or another lymphoproliferative disorder, including chronic lymphocytic leukemia and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, were excluded from the study. Patients diagnosed with chronic lymphocytic leukemia, mantle cell lymphoma, smoldering MM, Waldenström macroglobulinemia, smoldering Waldenström macroglobulinemia, or MM at their initial visit to the iStopMM clinic were also excluded from the study (n ¼ 613) (Figure).

Autoimmune Disease The presence of any autoimmune disorder was deﬁned as the exposure. This was determined by the presence of International Classiﬁcation of Diseases diagnosis codes for 42 autoimmune diseases at any time before or after the date of screening for MGUS was registered (Supplement Table 1, available at Annals. org). Autoimmune diseases were grouped together in the analyses on the basis of whether autoantibodies are a recognized feature of the disease. Diseases with autoantibodies were further divided into systemic and organ-speciﬁc diseases on the basis of the meth-odology of previous studies (Table 1) (12, 25–27). This categorization of autoimmune diseases is not based on rheumatologic deﬁnitions but is rooted in the hypothesis that chronic inﬂammation with autoantibody pro-duction increases the risk for clonal transformation and proliferation of plasma cells. It is included here to ensure comparability with the existing literature (12, 28). These autoimmune disease categories were evaluated for an association with MGUS. For patients with more than 1 autoimmune disease, conditions with antibodies were given precedence over those without, and those with systemic involvement over organ-speciﬁc conditions, so that each participant was included in only 1 of the 2 groups.

Statistical Analysis All statistical analyses were done in RStudio, ver-sion 4.3.1 (Posit Software). Univariate analyses using t tests and x 2 tests were used to compare differences in age, sex, and the prevalence of autoimmune diseases between groups. A Poisson regression model with log link was used to calculate the prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% CIs of MGUS among participants with and without autoimmune disorders in the iStopMM cohort, according to the groups described above.

Role of the Funding Source The funding sources had no role in the study’s design, conduct, or reporting.

### DISCUSSION

In this prospective, population-based study of more than 75000 persons systematically screened for MGUS, we found no association between autoimmune disease and MGUS. In sharp contrast, we did ﬁnd an association between autoimmune disorders and a prior clinical MGUS diagnosis. Our ﬁndings suggest that previously observed associations between autoimmune conditions and MGUS may have been affected by ascertainment bias. This has important clinical implications for the evaluation of patients with autoimmune disorders, as well as under-scoring the need for further studies of the cause of MGUS. We also found no differences between organ-speciﬁc and systemic autoimmune diseases or between antibody-positive and antibody-negative disorders. The observed association of Guillain–Barre syndrome with MGUS and IgM MGUS should be interpreted with cau-tion: Few patients had Guillain–Barre syndrome in these groups, and the multiple testing involved in scanning the 42 diseases for association may have given rise to spurious statistically signiﬁcant associations. Our ﬁndings are consistent with those of a previous screening study at the Mayo Clinic (22), which found no

association between autoimmune disease and MGUS that was systematically assessed. However, the cohort in that study was small and did not include LC-MGUS cases. Our current study offers more robust data based on a much larger cohort and a more detailed deﬁnition of MGUS cases, allowing stronger conclusions to be drawn from the results. Furthermore, the study from the Mayo Clinic did not provide information on a prior clinical diagnosis of MGUS and could therefore not distin-guish the association between autoimmune conditions and MGUS based on clinical detection in the course of usual care versus detection during systematic screening (22). Our null ﬁndings do not suggest that autoimmunity is a major cause or risk factor of MGUS; our results also do not support prior hypotheses that chronic inﬂammation causes clonal mutations in MGUS, or that the dysfunctional immune system observed in autoimmune disorders inadequately identiﬁes aberrant cells, leading to clonal proliferation (13). Our ﬁndings of an association between autoimmune diseases and clinical MGUS (detected in the course of usual care) as well as statistically signiﬁcant associations between several individual autoimmune diseases and clinical MGUS are in agreement with those of previous studies. This includes a study done in Sweden (12) that linked 14 autoimmune disorders to MGUS and a study of U.S. veterans (11) showing an association of 6 autoimmune disorders with MGUS. This is likely due to the clinical MGUS diagnosis being an incidental ﬁnding during evaluation of symptoms that are unrelated to MGUS but may be linked to an autoimmune disease, such as anemia, back pain, and elevated inﬂammatory markers. In addition, rheumatologists may have routinely screened for MGUS on the basis of results from previous studies showing an increased risk for MGUS and even subse-quent myeloma in patients with autoimmune diseases (15, 29). The sharp contrast to the lack of association with MGUS that is systematically assessed for could be explained by an ascertainment bias in studies focusing on only MGUS cases identiﬁed in usual care where routine screening is not implemented. Our results accordingly do not support the practice of routine screening for MGUS with serum protein electrophoresis or free light-chain analysis in persons with autoimmune diseases. Our results highlight the importance of this ascertainment bias that may have affected most previous studies on the association between MGUS and chronic diseases (21). Basing these studies only on clinical MGUS cohorts that are likely to have been to a substan-tial degree identiﬁed through work-up of the same diseases with which an association is being sought has led to a false association through reverse causality (12, 17, 18). A recent study similarly found an absence of association between osteoporosis and MGUS when MGUS was systematically ascertained, in contrast to prior work suggesting a relationship when MGUS was identiﬁed in the course of usual clinical care (30). The major strengths of this study lie in the large, screened cohort representing more than 50% of the entire population aged 40 years or older, the avail-ability of a subcohort with clinically diagnosed MGUS for comparison with the previous literature, and the accuracy and completeness of registers providing information on chronic disease diagnoses in Iceland (24). Nevertheless, the use of registered diagnoses is a potential limitation because the high accuracy of coding for chronic conditions shown in a validation study has not been demonstrated speciﬁcally for autoimmune diseases (24). An important consideration for the introduction of bias to the study is the recruitment process, because persons with previous health issues might be more inclined to participate. Indeed, the prevalence of autoimmune disorders in our data set is higher than in several previous epidemiologic study samples (31, 32) but similar to that of other population-based studies in Sweden (28, 29). Moreover, there is no reason to suspect a differential registration of autoimmune disorders between those with and without MGUS. More important, persons with a prior MGUS diagnosis might have been either more or less inclined

to participate in a screening study of the precursor condition of MM, depending on whether they believed they could beneﬁt from more care or believed the study did not apply to them. Nonetheless, the brochure accompanying the invitation emphasized the importance of broad participation and did not in any way discourage those with a prior MGUS diagnosis from participating. We have no indication of such differential participation of persons with prior MGUS in either direction. Of the 5 known risk factors for MGUS described in the introduction (pesticide exposure, family history, age, race, and sex), we adjusted for age and sex. Residual confounding may be present because we do not have data on pesticide exposure or race. The fact that the Icelandic population is nearly completely of Northern European descent makes the lack of adjust-ment for race an unlikely source of residual confounding, but it also means that the generalizability of our results may be limited outside similar populations. In this large, population-based screening study for MGUS, we did not ﬁnd an association between any autoimmune disorder and MGUS, in contrast to some prior studies. Our ﬁndings have direct clinical relevance because they suggest that recommendations to routinely screen patients with autoimmune disease for MGUS may not be warranted.
