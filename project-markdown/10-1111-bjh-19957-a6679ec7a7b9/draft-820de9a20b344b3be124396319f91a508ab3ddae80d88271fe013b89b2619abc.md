Summary Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is the asymptomatic precursor of multiple myeloma and related diseases but has also been associated with thrombosis. Prior studies have not been based on screened cohorts leading to bias. We assessed the risk of thrombosis in a cohort of 75 422 individuals over 40 years old who were screened for MGUS in Iceland. We also evaluated the association of M protein concentration with thrombotic risk. A total of 3668 participants had MGUS. After a median follow-­up of ~3.7 years, 124 venous and 252 arterial thrombotic events (10.3 and 21.0 per 1000 person years respectively) were observed in the MGUS group, compared to 1509 and 3471 in the non-­MGUS group (6.0 and 13.8 per 1000 person years respectively). M protein concentration was not associated with venous (p = 0.72) or arterial (p = 0.95) thrombosis. ### INTRODUCTION

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is an asymptomatic disorder characterized by the presence of monoclonal immunoglobulins (paraproteins) or free light chains (FLC) in the serum.1 MGUS is the precursor of multiple myeloma (MM) and related lymphoproliferative disorders with a risk of progression of 1%–1.5% per year.2–6 Although MGUS is often referred to as being asymptomatic until progression, it has been associated with various other disorders, including an increased risk of fractures,7,8 peripheral neuropathy,9,10 infections,11,12 kidney disease,13 as well as arterial and venous thrombotic events.14

Similar to other malignancies, individuals with MM appear to have a disease-­inherent increased risk of thrombosis15 and with the advent of novel therapies, such as im-munomodulatory agents, the risk of thrombosis in MM has increased significantly.16–18 MGUS, the precursor of MM, has also been associated with thrombosis in at least five studies from the US, Sweden and Denmark. Individuals with MGUS were found to have a 1.3-­ to 3.4-­fold increased risk of venous thromboembolism and about 1.5-­fold increased risk of arterial thrombotic events.14,19–22 Additional studies have found that the risk of thrombosis in MGUS is higher in those with a high M protein concentration, those who progress to ac-tive malignancy and those with other common thrombotic risk factors such as obesity and hypercholesterolaemia.23,24 Moreover, MGUS has been associated with a pro-­thrombotic state of increased levels of thrombin generation, prothrombotic phospholipid activity,25 fibrinogen, factor VIII and von Willebrand factor.26 However, all of these studies were based on individuals with MGUS where the diagnosis of MGUS was made incidentally during the work-­up of other unrelated diseases, including autoimmune disease, heart disease, en-docrine diseases and other disease that themselves increase the risk of thrombosis. We have recently shown that this selection bias may be so severe as to preclude meaningful interpretation of these prior studies.27 Furthermore, prior studies have not sufficiently addressed misclassification of MGUS in the lead-­up to MM diagnosis. In the only prior study based on a screened MGUS cohort from Olmsted county in the US, the 605 individuals who had MGUS only had an increased risk of sural phlebitis, popliteal artery embolism and femoral artery embolism but not with other thrombotic events.28 However, the observed events were few with two to four cases in the MGUS group. Therefore, there is a clear need for a large, prospective high-­ quality study based on a screened cohort with MGUS to

establish the association of MGUS and thrombotic risk. We were therefore motivated to study the risk of thrombosis in the large population-­based screened cohort with MGUS es-tablished in the Iceland Screens, Treats or Prevents Multiple Myeloma (iStopMM) study.

### METHODS

Study participants

Study participants and study data were acquired from the iStopMM study, a population-­based screening study for MGUS and randomized trial of follow-­up strategies. The study has been described in greater detail elsewhere.29 Briefly, all residents of Iceland born before 1976 in September 2016 were offered to participate and 80 759 (54% of the eligible population) subsequently provided their informed consent for screening and data collection. Individuals with a prior history of MM or lymphoproliferative disorders were excluded. Next, blood samples from 75 422 participants (93%) were, for the most part, passively obtained from study participants between 2016 and 2020 alongside blood sampling in the Icelandic healthcare system. The collected samples were screened for MGUS by serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and FLC assay. In cases of suspected M proteins of SPEP or an abnormal FLC assay result, immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE) was also performed. For this study, we included all screened participants of the iStopMM study.

Exposure and outcomes

Exposure was defined as having MGUS at the time of screening as determined by the presence of an M protein on IFE or abnormal FLC assay findings, adjusted for renal function.30 Participants with an FLC ratio \>100 (in-volved/uninvolved) or M protein concentration ≥30 g/L were excluded since they had advanced disease at screening. Further subgroup analyses were made for those with at least one M protein (heavy chain \[HC\] MGUS) and for those with light-­chain (LC) MGUS as well as for the different isotypes of M proteins (IgG and IgA, jointly denoted as non-­IgM, as well as IgM). ### Statistical analysis

We conducted a prospective follow-­up study using two primary methodologies. First, in a time-­to-­first-­event analysis, we followed participants from the time of MGUS screening to the time of their first thrombotic event as composite venous and arterial thrombosis and as the subtypes of venous and arterial thrombosis. Participants were censored at the time of death, end of follow-­up, MGUS progression to MM or related disorders or the occurrence of a thrombotic event other than the outcome of interest for that specific analysis. Cox regression with attained age as the underlying timescale was used to calculate hazard ratios (HR). Second, we modelled recurrent thrombotic events with mixed-­effects Cox models, assuming a subject-­level random intercept and a gap-­time timescale, so that event times were defined as time since screening or since the previous thrombotic event. Models were adjusted for age (as a non-­linear variable or as the underlying timescale) and sex as well as chronic infections, obesity and prior malignancy as potential risk factors for both thrombosis and MGUS. In a final primary model, we then also included prior thrombotic events as a proxy for unmeasured and unknown risk factors of both thrombosis and MGUS (see Supporting Information, Methods for further details). In an additional analysis, we modelled the association between serum M protein concentration and time to the first thrombotic event, in subjects with at least one detectable M protein. M protein concentration was modelled non-­linearly using restricted cubic splines, with 3–9 degrees of freedom, with the best-­fitting formulation picked according to the Bayesian information criterion. In cases of multiple detectable M protein, the highest concentration was used and non-­quantifiable M protein values \<0.1 g/L were assigned a random value below 0.1 g/L. Values were centred around the median M protein concentration.

Some of the participants with MGUS had previously known MGUS. This may have led to surveillance bias in a small portion of those with MGUS which could potentially drive a false association. We therefore conducted a sensitivity analysis where we excluded those who had a prior diagnosis of MGUS before the date of screening. The method of finding prior MGUS cases is described elsewhere.27

All statistical analyses were done using R.31 Cox models were fitted using the survival package32 and mixed-­effects Cox models with the coxme33 package.

### RESULTS

Of the 75 422 screened individuals, 3668 (4.9%) had MGUS. Those who had MGUS were older than those who did not have MGUS (Median age 69 vs. 61 years) and were more likely to be male (54% vs. 45%). Of those who had MGUS 2321 had non-­IgM MGUS, 719 had IgM MGUS, 313 had biclonal MGUS (here grouped with MGUS with M proteins \[i.e. HC MGUS\]) and 315 had LC MGUS (Table 1). The median follow-­up in the time-­to-­first-­event analysis was 3.67–3.84 years, depending on the outcome of interest. A total of 124 (10.3 per 1000 person-­years) and 1509 (6.0 per 1000 person-­years) venous thrombotic events and 252 (21.0 per 1000 person-­years) and 3471 (13.8 per 1000 person-­years) arterial thrombotic events were observed for those with and without MGUS respectively (Table S2). After adjusting for age, sex, obesity, chronic infections, prior malignancy and prior thrombosis, subjects with MGUS still had an increased rate of thrombosis overall (HR = 1.12; 95% confidence interval \[CI\]: 1.01–1.25). This finding was driven by venous thrombotic events (HR = 1.43; 95% CI: 1.19–1.73) and, in particular, by DVT (HR = 1.42; 95% CI: 1.09–1.86) but also PE (HR = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.05–1.76). Of the different MGUS subtypes, this association was observed in MGUS overall, MGUS with an M protein (HC MGUS) both with IgM and non-­IgM isotypes. Overall, we observed no evidence of an increased rate of arterial thrombosis in individuals with MGUS (HR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.87–1.13). However, for IgM MGUS, we observed a higher rate of arterial thrombotic events (HR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.03–1.70), in particular, peripheral arterial embolic events (HR = 5.21; 95% CI 1.03–14.59), but events were few with only four such events in the IgM MGUS group (Figure 1). In the recurrent event analysis, the median follow-­up time was 3.9 years. A total of 260 and 3092 venous events (20.2 and 11.7 per 1000 person-­years) and 263 and 3557 arterial events (20.4 and 13.5 per 1000 person-­years) were observed in those with and without MGUS respectively. Similar to the time-­to-­first-­event analysis, we observed an increased rate of venous thrombosis in individuals with MGUS and HC MGUS (HR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.03–1.68 and HR = 1.36 95% CI: 1.06–1.76 respectively). ### DISCUSSION

In this large population-­based screening study including over 75 000 participants, we found that MGUS is associated with venous thrombotic events. This is consistent with multiple previous studies that have found that individuals with MGUS have 1.3-­ to 3.4-­fold risk of venous thrombosis.14,19–22 However, these prior studies have all been based on incidentally diagnosed cohorts with MGUS where selection bias has made the results ques-tionable.27 This may explain why the observed HR for venous thrombosis observed in this study is at the low end

Interestingly, M protein concentration was not found to be associated with a risk of any type of thrombotic event. This is somewhat surprising as some have suggested that increased viscosity or hypercoagulability of near-­malignancy in those with a high M protein concentration contributes to thrombosis in MGUS.34 In fact, an M protein concentration \>15 g/L has been associated with an increased risk of thrombosis among those with MGUS.23,24 However, these prior studies did not account for MGUS progression or subsequent treatment and it is possible that this may have led to an observed increased risk of thrombosis in this group which is at the highest risk of progression. Cases have been described of thrombogenic monoclonal immunoglobulins in MGUS like anti-­PF4 antibodies leading to a thrombocytopenic and thrombotic state similar to heparin-­induced thrombo-cytopenia or vaccine-­induced thrombotic thrombocytope-nia.35,36 In light of the findings of this study, it is unlikely that disease activity or M protein bulk is the main driver of thrombotic risk in MGUS. Instead, a possible explanation could be the affinity of M proteins, in some individuals with MGUS, to PF4 or other thrombogenic auto-­antigens. Such a high-­risk subgroup, if it could be identified, might benefit from anti-­coagulation, or as has recently been described, could require anti-­myeloma therapy to prevent debilitating thrombotic events.37 Prior studies on coagulation markers in MGUS have been based on biased cohorts25,26 and studies including a screened cohort are warranted to further under-stand the pathobiology of thrombotic risk in MGUS and to identify a potential subpopulation with MGUS and a high risk of thrombosis. This study has several strengths. First, this is a large population-­based screening study, circumventing the selection bias inherent in most other studies on MGUS that include incidentally diagnosed cohorts. Second, a majority of those with MGUS underwent diagnostic evaluation at baseline and follow-­up during the study period and progression events are well recorded limiting the effect of later anti-­myeloma therapy on thrombotic risk. Third, as all study participants were screened for MGUS, the control group has also been tested and shown to not have MGUS, unlike most prior studies. Finally, the Icelandic Healthcare Registries used in the study cover all inpatient care as well as the majority of outpatient visits in the country, including all emer-gency room visits. Given the severe nature of thrombotic events, the data are likely to include virtually all clinically meaningful thrombotic events occurring to study participants and missing data are likely to be negligible. This study also has some important limitations. First, data on thrombotic events are based on diagnoses made in the Icelandic healthcare system, relying on accurate detection and registration of these thrombotic events by Icelandic phy-sicians. We have shown that the validity of chronic disease diagnoses made in these registries is ≥95%,38 but the precise validity of acute disease diagnoses is not known. Second, even though this is the largest study on MGUS and thrombosis in a screened population to date, it may have been underpow-ered to detect rare thrombotic events or differences in smaller

subgroups with MGUS, where associations may be spurious (e.g. for IgM and LC MGUS). Third, some thrombotic events may have come to light during MGUS follow-­up leading to surveillance bias. However, most thrombotic events are not subclinical and would have been detected even if the individ-ual is not in follow-­up. Fourth, due to the nature of the study, a non-­causal relationship between MGUS and thrombosis, for example, due to shared risk factors, cannot be ruled out. Finally, the study population is, like the Icelandic population, genetically homogenous and mostly white which may limit generalization to other populations. In conclusion, in this large population-­based study including around 75 000 individuals screened for MGUS, we found that MGUS is associated with a 1.4-­fold increased risk of venous thrombosis but not with arterial thrombosis. This appears not to be related to the M protein concentration, which in the setting of the prior literature suggests that there may be a subpopulation of individuals with MGUS with a pro-­thrombotic phenotype that is independent of the underlying malignant process, that are driving this increased rate of thrombosis. Further studies are needed to uncover the underlying mechanisms and identify the potential subgroup with monoclonal gammopathy of thrombotic significance. By doing so, we may identify a group that could benefit from intervention, improving their survival and quality of life and improving the care of individuals with MGUS overall.
